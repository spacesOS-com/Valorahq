Social Security claiming: read a break-even estimate without mistaking it for a decision

A break-even calculation compares two claiming ages and shows when the total received from the later start passes the total from the earlier one. That is a useful fact. It is not a recommendation, and it leaves out most of what decides a real claiming month. This guide explains what Valora's Social Security break-even calculator does, checks its assumptions against Social Security Administration (SSA) pages, and gives a comparison sheet for the questions a calculator cannot answer. It does not name a best age and does not say that waiting is always better, or worse.

Key takeaways

  • The SSA says you can start retirement benefits as early as 62, but the amount is reduced. For people born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67, and starting at 62 gives 70.0% of the full benefit.
  • Delayed retirement credits raise the benefit for each month you wait past full retirement age. The SSA says the increase stops at 70, and that the 8% yearly rate applies to people born in 1943 or later.
  • A break-even age comes from adding up payments. It does not predict how long anyone will live.
  • Leaving work, claiming benefits, the month a payment arrives and Medicare sign-up are four separate events.
  • Your own SSA estimate, not a calculator's example, is your starting number.

This guide is for US readers. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. No claiming age is recommended here.

What the break-even calculator does

Valora's Social Security break-even calculator takes one number from you, your estimated monthly benefit at full retirement age of 67. It sets the benefit at 62 to 70% of that number and the benefit at 70 to 124%. It then adds up payments in whole-year steps and shows the first full year in which the total from claiming at 70 is larger than the total from claiming at 62.

Its own note says it ignores cost-of-living adjustments, taxes, spousal benefits and the time value of money. It also assumes a full retirement age of 67 and compares only two starting ages, 62 and 70.

Check the factors against the SSA

Full retirement age. The SSA's page for people born in 1960 or later says full retirement age is 67, and gives the share of the full benefit for each month from 62 up to 67. At 62 it is 70.0%. At 66 it is 93.3%. At 67 it is 100.0%. The SSA says that if your birthday is on the 1st of the month, it figures your benefit as if your birthday were in the previous month. It also says that if you are eligible as a survivor, your full retirement age may be different.

Early claiming. The SSA's Early or Late Retirement page says a benefit is reduced 5/9 of one percent for each month before full retirement age, up to 36 months, and then 5/12 of one percent for each month after that. For 60 months before a full retirement age of 67, the SSA says the reduction is 30 percent.

Delayed claiming. The SSA says delayed retirement credits are given for each month you wait past full retirement age, and that the increase stops at 70. It lists the yearly rate by birth year up to 8.0% for people born in 1943 or later; earlier birth years have lower rates. For a person born in 1960 or later, waiting three years from 67 to 70 therefore adds 24%, which is the 124% in the calculator. The SSA also says that if you start before 70, some of the credits are not applied until the January after you start, and that its own calculator shows the amount with all credits applied, for comparison.

Why the crossover is not a prediction

A break-even age is where two running totals meet. It does not estimate survival, and it does not include a spouse. The SSA says the choice of when to start is personal and that the month you pick could mean higher payments for you and your family. The calculator has no way to see your family.

Valora's calculator works in whole years and shows the first full year in which claiming later comes out ahead. It does not show the month. The fictional example below shows what that means.

A fictional example

Jordan, born in 1962, has an SSA estimate of $2,400 a month at full retirement age of 67. This is the calculator's default number, and it is invented for this example. It is not a typical benefit.

  • Claiming at 62 gives 70.0% of $2,400, or $1,680 a month.
  • Claiming at 70 gives 124% of $2,400, or $2,976 a month.
  • Valora's calculator shows "Age 81" as the break-even age for these two figures.
  • Adding the payments month by month, without any adjustment, gives a slightly earlier crossover. After 220 months of payments from the 62 start, the totals are $369,600 at 62 and $369,024 at 70. After 221 months, they are $371,280 and $372,000. So the totals cross about 80 years and 5 months after the 62 start. The calculator shows "Age 81" because 81 is the first full year in which the later start is ahead. A crossover at 80 years and 1 month would also show "Age 81".

That is arithmetic about two straight lines. It does not say what Jordan should do. It does not include cost-of-living adjustments, tax, a spouse's benefit or the years a person actually lives.

Four events that are not the same

  • Leaving work. The SSA's reporting page says work earnings before full retirement age can affect your benefit payment, and that you should report changes. It does not say that leaving work starts your benefit. This guide does not explain the earnings test or its limits. Ask the SSA.
  • Claiming benefits. The month you choose to start, with the amount that follows.
  • Payment month. The SSA says benefits are generally paid in the month after the effective month.
  • Medicare enrollment. The SSA says that if you delay retirement, you should sign up for Medicare at 65, because in some circumstances your coverage may be delayed and cost more.

What this guide leaves out

It does not explain spousal or survivor benefits, the earnings test, how benefits are taxed or the effect of working while you claim. Each depends on current rules that this guide did not read. Ask the SSA or a qualified adviser about them.

Documents to gather

  • Your SSA retirement estimate from your own record.
  • Your birth date, to check full retirement age.
  • The start months you are comparing.
  • A note on whether you or a spouse is still working.
  • Your questions for the SSA.

Bottom line

A break-even age compares two running totals and nothing else. Use it as one input beside your own SSA estimate, your health coverage dates and your household. The SSA, or a qualified professional who has your facts, can answer the rest.

Common questions

What does the 62-versus-70 break-even calculation show?

The first age at which the total from claiming at 70 passes the total from claiming at 62, using the stated assumptions. It is not a prediction of how long you will live.

Is waiting until 70 always better?

Valora does not say so, and none of the SSA pages it read say so. The answer depends on facts a break-even calculation leaves out. Ask the SSA or a qualified adviser about your situation.

Does the 8% credit apply to everyone?

The SSA says the 8% yearly credit applies to people born in 1943 or later. Earlier birth years have lower rates.

Does claiming Social Security start Medicare?

Medicare enrollment is a separate step. The SSA says that if you delay Social Security, you should still sign up for Medicare at 65.

What should I send through the inquiry form?

Describe the question you want to work through, such as which start months you are comparing. Do not submit your Social Security statement, account numbers or other confidential financial documents.
