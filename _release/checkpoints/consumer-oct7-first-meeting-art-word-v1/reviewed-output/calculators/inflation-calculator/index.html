Inflation calculator

See what a dollar amount today is worth in the future — or was worth in the past — at a given inflation rate.

Equivalent future purchasing power

—

Illustrative fixed-rate model: amount × (1 + assumed annual inflation rate)^years. The default 3% is an example, not a measured inflation rate or a forecast. This tool does not retrieve live or historical inflation data. Actual inflation and household costs can vary.

This calculator estimates the future dollar amount equivalent to an amount today if one assumed annual inflation rate applies throughout the period. Enter an amount, a number of years and an assumed annual percentage rate. The result is a purchasing-power illustration, not an investment balance, a forecast or a report of measured inflation.

The tool multiplies today's amount by (1 + assumed annual inflation rate)^years. It does not fetch current prices or historical inflation data. The rate you enter is an assumption; the tool does not verify it.

Key takeaways

  • Use an assumed rate, not an unlabeled claim about future inflation.
  • The output is a future equivalent amount, not investment earnings.
  • Keep the amount, years and rate beside the displayed result.
  • A household's costs can differ from a broad price measure.

How to use the inflation calculator

First, enter the dollar amount whose purchasing power you want to compare. Next, enter the number of years in the hypothetical period. Finally, enter an assumed annual inflation percentage. Save all three inputs with the output and a date so the scenario is understandable later.

Change one input at a time to see what changes the model. A longer period or a different assumed rate changes the estimate without showing what prices will actually do. Do not choose a spending or investment decision solely because one scenario shows a larger number.

Use nonnegative amounts and years for an ordinary forward comparison, and keep the assumed rate above -100% so the model's growth factor is positive. This is an interpretation boundary, not a claim that the current form validates every case. The served script converts empty or unparseable fields to zero and does not fully check all unusual mathematical inputs. An unexpected display is not a meaningful personal-finance answer.

A worked example with a stated assumption

For an illustrative $10,000 amount, 20 years and a constant assumed annual inflation rate of 3%, the calculation is $10,000 × 1.03^20. The unrounded value is about $18,061.11, and the displayed amount rounds to $18,061. Under those assumptions, that future amount would have the modeled purchasing power of $10,000 today.

The example does not mean a $10,000 account grows to $18,061. It describes a hypothetical change in the dollar amount needed for equivalent purchasing power. It does not include an investment return, interest, deposits or withdrawals. At a zero assumed rate or a zero-year period, the modeled equivalent amount equals the starting amount.

A 3% rate here is an example, not today's inflation reading or a forecast. If you use a different assumption, label its source and what it represents. The model repeats one rate; it cannot show a sequence of different annual inflation rates.

Talk it through

Distinguish this assumption model from historical CPI comparisons

The Bureau of Labor Statistics explanation of its inflation calculator says that its historical tool uses the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, US city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted. Valora's tool does not retrieve that series or compare two observed CPI values. Do not present this output as a BLS historical result.

The BLS purchasing-power explanation describes using CPI ratios to compare purchasing power across dates. That is a separate method based on observed index data. Here, the user supplies a fixed rate and number of years.

A broad measure is not a list of your household's actual bills. Keep specific costs and payment dates separate from the model. For an income change, the household income change guide separates confirmed payments, expected receipts and commitments. A purchasing-power illustration does not resolve those timing questions.

Keep a private assumptions worksheet

Record six fields: the scenario and date; today's amount; years; assumed annual rate and source; displayed future equivalent amount; and the question the model does not answer. Keep supporting records private. The blank CSV is an original record aid, not a budget recommendation.

If comparing two scenarios, retain both sets of inputs and explain which assumption changed. Do not treat the difference as a guaranteed cost increase or as money you will receive. The display rounds to whole dollars, which can hide small differences between unrounded results.

Related guidance and limits

The retirement savings growth calculator models a balance with contributions and an assumed return. That is a different question from the purchasing-power comparison here. An accumulation output before inflation should not be relabeled as an after-inflation spending amount without a separate, clearly stated comparison.

The savings duration guide explains a fixed annual withdrawal model. It does not automatically increase withdrawals for inflation. Keep the separate tools' assumptions visible rather than joining their outputs into an unstated retirement plan.

This inflation tool does not model household-specific price changes, variable inflation, income, taxes, investment returns or a payment calendar. It is an educational scenario. If asking for professional help, describe the assumptions and the decision you want reviewed, then compare written scope and costs. Inflation calculator questions

What does this calculator estimate?

A future dollar amount equivalent to an amount today under one assumed annual inflation rate and period. It is not an investment balance or forecast.

Does the tool use live CPI data?

No. You enter a fixed assumed rate. The tool does not retrieve current prices or historical CPI values.

What does the worked example show?

With $10,000, 20 years and an assumed constant 3% annual rate, the model rounds the future equivalent amount to $18,061. The rate is illustrative, not a forecast.

What happens at a zero rate or zero-year period?

The modeled equivalent amount equals the starting amount. No modeled price-level increase is applied.

Can this result explain my household bills?

No. It does not model household-specific costs, income or payment dates. Keep those records and questions separate.

Does the form validate every unusual input?

No. The current script converts empty or unparseable fields to zero and does not fully validate all mathematical cases. Unsupported results should not drive a decision.
