Retirement planning: map your income, timing and costs before you choose help

Retirement planning is easier to compare and discuss when you first write down where retirement income will come from, which dates affect it, and who is responsible for each decision. Social Security, health coverage, withdrawals from retirement accounts and any pension each have their own rules and timing. A single "retirement number" cannot stand in for them.

Key takeaways

  • List your income sources and their start dates before asking anyone what you "need".
  • Social Security, Medicare and required withdrawals each have official timing rules. Check them at the source for your birth date.
  • A calculator shows one defined assumption, not a plan. Keep its limits next to its output.
  • Decide who owns each question: the tax calculation, the investment decision, the benefit claim and the health-coverage sign-up are different jobs.

This guide is for US readers preparing for or reviewing retirement. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. No verified roster, availability, return forecast, claiming age or withdrawal amount is offered here.

What decision are you actually making?

Start with the specific choice in front of you. "Am I ready to retire?" is too broad to check against any source. A narrower question can be investigated.

You may be deciding when to stop working full time, when to start Social Security, how to handle workplace health coverage until Medicare, whether to take pension income as monthly payments or a lump sum, or how to begin withdrawing from savings. These decisions touch each other, but each has its own deadlines and its own source of rules.

Write the decision in one sentence, name the date it needs to be made, and name the person who can supply the missing facts. If the choice is only a possibility, keep that visible. A plan built on a date that has not been decided is a draft, and it is fine to label it that way.

Build a retirement-income map

An income map puts each source of retirement money next to the rule that controls when and how it starts. It is an original worksheet, not a government form or a financial plan.

Fill the table in pencil. Write "not confirmed" where you do not have evidence, and keep it for your own use rather than sending account details through any web form. An income map with open rows is useful because it shows what the next conversation has to settle.

What does Social Security timing depend on?

It depends on the age at which you start and on your full retirement age, which the SSA sets by birth year. Use the SSA's pages, not a rule of thumb.

The SSA says you can start retirement benefits as early as age 62, but you are entitled to full benefits only when you reach full retirement age. If you start early, the benefit is reduced for each month before full retirement age. If you delay past full retirement age, up to age 70, the amount increases through delayed retirement credits. For people born in 1960 or later, the SSA lists full retirement age as 67. For earlier birth years it is lower, rising by a few months per birth year.

The SSA also says each person's situation is different and that you will need to consider factors that may affect your planning. Those factors are yours to identify, with a qualified professional if you use one. This guide does not suggest a claiming age.

If you want to see how claiming timing changes a benefit, Valora's Social Security break-even calculator and delayed retirement credit calculator are available. Read their stated limits first. The break-even calculator, for example, ignores cost-of-living adjustments, taxes, spousal benefits and the time value of money, so its output is not a recommendation.

When does Medicare sign-up fall relative to retirement?

Medicare has its own sign-up window, which does not depend on when you claim Social Security. Medicare.gov says your first chance to sign up is generally when you turn 65, in a seven-month Initial Enrollment Period that starts three months before you turn 65 and ends three months after the month you turn 65.

Medicare.gov also says that if you miss that window you may have to wait to sign up and may pay a monthly late enrollment penalty for as long as you have Part B coverage. Special Enrollment Periods exist for certain situations, so the exact answer depends on your coverage at the time. Medicare.gov says people who have or had health insurance through their own job or a spouse's job can qualify for a Special Enrollment Period, which starts the first month after the Initial Enrollment Period ends and has its own end date, and that it does not apply while you are still in your Initial Enrollment Period. Medicare.gov says the end of COBRA or retiree coverage does not qualify you for this Special Enrollment Period; COBRA is not considered group health plan coverage.

The SSA says that if you decide to delay Social Security benefits until after age 65, you should still apply for Medicare within three months of your 65th birthday, because Part B and Part D may cost more if you wait longer. The SSA's three-month instruction is general, and employer coverage can change the timing. Check the Medicare.gov and SSA pages for your own timing, and ask your employer's benefits office what happens to workplace coverage when you leave.

How do withdrawals from retirement accounts work?

Different accounts follow different rules, and the tax effect is a separate question from the investment decision. The IRS explains the required minimum distribution (RMD) rules in plain terms.

The IRS says you cannot keep retirement funds in a traditional IRA, SIMPLE IRA, SEP IRA or workplace plan indefinitely. Its RMD pages say you generally must start taking withdrawals at age 73, and the final regulations in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2024-33 say the applicable age is set by date of birth and is 75 for people born in 1960 or later. The IRS FAQ also says: "Participants in a workplace retirement plan (for example, 401(k) or profit-sharing plan) can delay taking their RMDs until the year they retire, unless they're a 5% owner of the business sponsoring the plan." Confirm your own age and your first withdrawal date on the IRS pages.

The IRS also says RMDs are not required from Roth IRAs, or from designated Roth accounts in a 401(k) or 403(b) plan, while the owner is alive. Withdrawals are generally included in taxable income except for any part that was taxed before or that can be received tax-free. The required amount is the prior year-end balance divided by a distribution period from an IRS table.

Valora's RMD estimate calculator uses the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table and says it does not cover Roth IRAs or the joint-life table used in some spouse situations. The withdrawal amount calculator is arithmetic, a portfolio times a rate, and does not recommend a rate. The how long your savings will last calculator assumes a constant withdrawal and a constant return, and real markets and spending vary.

Talk it through

What should you ask before choosing pension payments or a lump sum?

Ask the plan administrator for the actual terms in writing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists deciding when to take Social Security and how to use a pension as two of the most important retirement-income decisions, and it publishes tips for people considering a lump-sum payout. It notes that a lump sum gives flexibility about whether to invest or save, and that there are risks too.

Questions worth putting in writing: What does each option pay, and for how long? What happens to a spouse or other survivor? What does the plan exclude? Who can I contact if the figures change? Compare answers from the plan's documents, not from a summary.

A fictional example: separating the jobs

Riley and Sam are considering Riley's last day of work. Riley has workplace health coverage, a pension offered as payments or a lump sum, and a traditional 401(k). Sam has not yet looked at Social Security records.

Instead of asking whether they can retire, they write four dated questions: when to request the pension terms, when Riley's workplace coverage ends and when Medicare sign-up applies, what Sam's own SSA records show, and when withdrawals from the 401(k) would begin and who will calculate the tax effect. Each question has an owner and a place to record the answer.

This example is invented. It contains no recommended retirement date, claiming age, withdrawal rate or tax result. It shows why one number cannot answer all four questions.

How do you compare professionals who offer retirement help?

Compare what each professional will do in writing, how they are paid, and what they leave to you. Investor.gov says brokers and investment advisers offer a variety of services at a variety of prices, and that it pays to comparison shop. It says you should feel free to ask how and how much your adviser is being paid, and that if a fee is quoted as a percentage, you should understand what that is in dollars.

Investor.gov also says the most important question to consider before hiring an investment professional is whether the person is registered with the SEC or a state securities regulator, and it points to a free search tool. It describes the firm's relationship summary, known as Form CRS, which covers services, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, the standard of conduct and any reportable disciplinary history.

Registration is a starting check, not an endorsement. Ask which retirement tasks are included: planning only, ongoing investment management, tax coordination or all of these. Ask what is excluded and who is responsible for each deliverable. For the account-by-account comparison of scope and costs, see our guide to managing investments.

Prepare for a first meeting without oversharing

You can have a useful first conversation without sending account numbers, tax returns or benefit statements through a public form. Bring the income map with open rows marked, your decision sentence and your dates. Agree on a secure way to share documents with whoever you hire, and share only what the stated task needs.

What can this guide not decide?

It cannot say when you should retire, when to start Social Security, whether to take a pension as a lump sum, how much you can withdraw or which professional to hire. Those decisions depend on facts only you and the professionals you engage can verify. Nothing here forecasts a result or promises one.

Conclusion: put dates and owners ahead of a single number

Retirement planning becomes manageable when each source of income and each cost has a date, a rule you have checked at the source and an owner. Use the income map to show what is confirmed and what is still open. Let the official pages decide the timing rules, and let qualified professionals work on the questions that depend on your facts.

Common questions

What does the income map in this guide cover?

It covers sources of retirement income and their start dates, health coverage, spending and the tax effect of withdrawals. The map organizes these questions. It does not decide the answer to any of them.

When can I start Social Security retirement benefits?

The SSA says as early as age 62, with reduced benefits if you start before full retirement age. Full retirement age depends on birth year and is 67 for people born in 1960 or later. Delaying past full retirement age, up to age 70, increases the benefit.

When do I have to sign up for Medicare?

Medicare.gov says your Initial Enrollment Period generally lasts seven months around your 65th birthday: it starts three months before you turn 65 and ends three months after the month you turn 65. Missing it can lead to a late enrollment penalty. Special Enrollment Periods exist for some situations.

When do required withdrawals start?

The IRS says you generally must start taking withdrawals from traditional IRAs and workplace plan accounts at age 73. The final regulations in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2024-33 say the applicable age depends on date of birth and is 75 for people born in 1960 or later. The IRS FAQ also says participants in a workplace plan such as a 401(k) can delay RMDs until the year they retire, unless they are a 5% owner of the sponsoring business. Confirm your own date and rules on the IRS pages.

Do Roth accounts have required withdrawals?

The IRS says no RMDs are required from Roth IRAs, or from designated Roth accounts in a 401(k) or 403(b) plan, while the owner is alive. Beneficiaries are subject to RMD rules.

Can a calculator tell me if I can retire?

No. A calculator applies one defined assumption. Each Valora calculator states what it leaves out. Treat the output as an illustration to discuss, not a finding about your situation.
