Financial advisor for business owners: choose the work first

A financial advisor for a business owner should connect the company, the household and the owner's next decision. That may mean deciding how much cash can leave the business, comparing retirement plans for employees, or testing whether a possible sale would fund retirement. Start by naming the decision you need help with. Then ask candidates to show their process, fees and responsibilities before you hire them.

Key takeaways

  • Name the decision before choosing an advisor.
  • Separate operating cash, accessible household assets and estimated company value.
  • Compare fees, responsibilities and downside scenarios in writing.

This guide is for owners of US businesses. It is an educational selection framework, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. It does not contain a verified list of business-owner specialists. You can use the worksheet and interview questions below with professionals you find through your own research or existing relationships.

Choose for the decision, not the label

“Business-owner specialist” is a starting claim to investigate, not a work plan. An advisor who helps owners invest sale proceeds may not be the right person to coordinate a sale that has not happened. A planner who works with sole proprietors may have little experience with a company that has partners and employees. Ask how their work changes with your stage, ownership structure and source of income.

Write a one-sentence brief before the first call: “I want to know whether I can reduce my hours without depending on a sale,” or “I need to compare the household impact of reinvesting versus taking distributions.” A specific brief makes it easier to compare proposals. If a candidate immediately recommends a portfolio before understanding that question, ask what problem the portfolio is meant to solve.

  • Building the business: look for a process for variable income, accessible reserves, personal guarantees and savings outside the company.
  • Employing people or sharing ownership: ask how the advisor coordinates retirement-plan administration, partner agreements and household planning.
  • Approaching a transfer: ask for a comparison of sale, partial sale, succession and continued ownership, including a delayed or cancelled transaction.

Put the household and the company on separate worksheets

The business may support your family without all its assets being available to the family. Cash needed for payroll, tax, equipment or a debt covenant has a different job from personal savings. Likewise, a business valuation is not a checking-account balance. An advisor should make those differences visible rather than combining every number into one reassuring net-worth total.

Use two worksheets and a third list of connections between them. The household worksheet records spending, liquid savings, personal debt, retirement accounts and insurance. The company worksheet records operating cash, debt, expected capital spending and an estimate of ownership value. The connections list records owner pay, distributions, guarantees and any family money lent to the company. Mark estimates as estimates and attach the date and source.

A first-pass inventory

  • Available now: cash you can access, where it is held and which bills it must cover.
  • Restricted or committed: tax reserves, business operating needs, collateral and funds subject to contractual limits.
  • Estimated value: ownership interests and the assumptions behind a valuation.
  • Contingent obligations: guarantees, partner commitments and payments that depend on future events.
  • Upcoming decisions: a hiring plan, renewal, loan maturity, retirement-plan election or potential transaction.

This is a preparation tool, not a reason to upload confidential documents to a directory. Share detailed statements through a secure channel only after checking the professional and agreeing on the work. Ask whether their written plan will retain these distinctions, including a downside case where business income falls while household expenses continue.

Three owners, three different assignments

The following examples are fictional. They show how the question changes; they are not client stories or expected results.

The growing owner with uneven cash flow

An owner wants to hire and expand but draws personal income irregularly. The first assignment is a cash-flow map: when receipts arrive, which obligations come first, what the household needs and how a slower period would be funded. Ask the advisor and accountant how they distinguish cash in the bank from money available for distributions. Investing a surplus matters only after deciding whether it really is a surplus.

The partner whose risks sit outside the balance sheet

A co-owner has savings but also a personal guarantee and an agreement to purchase a departing partner's interest. The assignment is to identify obligations, trigger events and funding gaps with counsel. An advisor can model the household effects and coordinate a review, but should not substitute a financial projection for an attorney's interpretation of the agreement or a licensed professional's insurance analysis.

The owner who wants to retire after a sale

An owner has an estimated business value but no binding transaction. The assignment is to compare what retirement requires with several possible proceeds scenarios. Ask what changes if the price is lower, some proceeds are deferred or no buyer appears. A good proposal states the assumptions and the choices that remain available, rather than treating a hoped-for closing as guaranteed cash.

Ask how cash moves out of the business

The right owner-pay approach depends on the entity, tax treatment, financing terms and actual business needs. Ask your CPA to explain the rules that apply to salary, distributions or draws in your case. The advisor's role is to connect the resulting household income to spending, saving and risk, not to announce a universal percentage of revenue you should take home.

Include taxes in the cash-flow conversation. The IRS estimated-tax guidance explains that taxes generally need to be paid as income is earned or received through withholding or estimated payments, and that inadequate or late payments can create penalties. Ask who updates the estimate when income changes, who schedules payments and which records support the calculation. A plan that says “set aside tax” without an owner and a review date leaves work undone.

Ask for a practical rule to revisit, not a permanent promise: what information would allow another distribution, and what would require pausing one? The answer might involve operating costs, loan requirements or planned spending. Those inputs must come from your business records and advisers, not a generic online reserve target.

Compare retirement plans with employee costs included

A retirement plan is a business commitment as well as a personal savings tool. Before comparing a SEP, SIMPLE IRA, 401(k) or defined-benefit arrangement, identify employee eligibility, compensation, contribution obligations, administration and how reliably the business can fund the plan. Ask for costs to the company and services to employees, not only a projection of the owner's deduction.

The IRS describes a one-participant 401(k) as covering an owner with no employees, or the owner and spouse. When eligible employees are hired, inclusion and other plan requirements change. If you also contribute through another employer, annual elective-deferral limits apply by person, not separately to each plan. Have a qualified plan professional check eligibility and current-year limits.

The IRS overview of self-employed retirement plans describes several plan types, including cash-balance arrangements within the defined-benefit category. Do not assume a high-income owner automatically benefits from one. Funding, administration and actuarial work are part of the comparison. Ask what happens to the commitment in a weaker year and whether the design fits the rest of your finances.

Test a sale before building retirement around it

A sale-price estimate is useful, but it is not the amount you can spend. Put purchase price, debt repayment, transaction fees, estimated taxes, escrow, contingent payments and retained ownership on separate lines. Identify what is received at closing and what depends on future events. Ask your deal team to verify the terms; the household plan should use the money actually available under each scenario.

The IRS explains that a business sale can involve multiple assets with different tax treatment. A financial advisor should coordinate with qualified tax and legal professionals before assuming a single tax rate or choosing a transaction structure. The SBA's sale guidance also discusses valuation approaches and the need for an attorney-reviewed sales agreement.

Request three written cases: the contemplated transaction, a less favorable outcome and continued ownership. You and your advisers choose the assumptions; no fixed valuation discount fits every business. Compare household income needs, liquid assets, remaining exposure and decisions such as working longer or adjusting spending. The point is to learn which decision changes when the deal changes.

If selling is not your goal, say so. Passing the business to family, bringing in management or holding it for income can require different legal, tax and operating work. An advisor should help clarify your objective and coordinate the appropriate professionals, not make a sale the default because liquid assets are easier to manage.

Build outside wealth with the risks in view

Owning a business can link your income, career and a large part of your assets to one outcome. Savings outside the company can provide another source of funds, but the amount and investment mix depend on your time horizon, liquidity needs and willingness and ability to take risk. Do not treat “outside the business” as a synonym for safe: securities can lose value, and account protection does not guarantee investment performance.

Investor.gov explains diversification as spreading money among investments to reduce risk. Ask the advisor to assess the household's total exposure, including your private company, rather than showing a diversified securities portfolio while ignoring the business. Ask how their plan would respond to a business downturn and a market decline happening together.

Get responsibilities and fees in writing

Ask who owns each task. The financial advisor may lead household planning and investment advice; the CPA or other qualified tax professional handles tax analysis within their engagement; counsel handles legal documents and deal interpretation; a valuation or transaction professional may assess and market the business. Roles can overlap, so the written scope matters more than the job title.

Ask for the full proposed annual cost in dollars using your expected circumstances. Separate planning fees, investment-management fees, product or fund costs, transaction work and third-party administration. If an advisor charges on managed assets, ask whether they work with owners whose value is mostly illiquid and whether transferring assets is required. A project fee and an ongoing engagement serve different purposes; compare the work rather than declaring one structure better for everyone.

Investor.gov's adviser guide explains that services and fees depend on the agreement and encourages reading the relationship summary and Form ADV brochure. Check registration and disclosures, ask about conflicts and referral compensation, and understand which services require a separate engagement. Registration is a check, not an endorsement or proof of business-owner experience.

Use these questions in the first meeting

  1. Which owner decisions like mine have you handled, and what work did you personally do?
  2. What information would you need to separate company cash from household resources?
  3. How would you test a plan that depends on a future sale?
  4. Who checks debt guarantees, partner agreements and tax assumptions?
  5. What retirement-plan comparison would you prepare for my employee situation?
  6. Can you show a sample plan or action list with private information removed?
  7. What is included in the fee, what costs extra and who monitors deadlines?
  8. What would we complete first, and how would we decide whether continuing the engagement is useful?

Look for explanations you can test: a list of inputs, a deliverable, an assigned owner and a review date. Be cautious about a guaranteed sale value, promised investment return or tax saving offered before the professional has examined your facts. A good first conversation can end with a smaller assignment or a referral to another specialist.

Prepare for the conversation without sending everything

Start with a short summary of ownership, the business's stage, personal goals and the decision that brought you to the meeting. Then prepare recent business financial statements, a household spending estimate, account summaries, debt and guarantee documents, existing retirement-plan information and relevant partner or transaction agreements. Ask which documents are actually needed and how they will be protected before sharing them.

After comparing candidates, write down the decision, selected scope, cost and next checkpoint. A useful plan leaves you knowing what gets done next and who is responsible. Common questions

Do I need a business-owner specialist or a general financial advisor?

Start with the decision you need help making. A general planner may fit household budgeting and investments; complex partner arrangements, employee plans or a transaction may require relevant experience and coordination with separate tax, legal and business professionals. Ask for the work process and scope rather than relying on a specialist label.

What should I ask when most of my wealth is in the business?

Ask how the advisor separates accessible household assets from operating cash, restrictions and estimated ownership value. Request a downside case that includes lower business income and a delayed or cancelled sale. Get fees and any requirement to transfer investment assets in writing.

Can I use a solo 401(k) if I employ other people?

Do not assume so. IRS guidance describes a one-participant 401(k) as covering an owner with no employees, or the owner and spouse. Eligible employees must be included under applicable plan rules, and other requirements may change. Have a qualified plan professional review eligibility and current-year limits.

Is my business sale price the amount available for retirement?

No. The household plan needs to distinguish price from closing cash, debt repayments, fees, taxes, escrow, contingent payments and retained equity. Tax treatment can differ across assets in a business sale. Ask qualified transaction, tax and legal professionals to verify the terms before planning from net proceeds.

Does this page list verified business-owner advisors?

No. This is an educational guide to choosing work and assessing a professional. It does not present a verified specialist roster or promise advisor availability or introductions.

What should I bring to the first conversation?

Begin with your ownership structure, goals and the decision you need help making. Prepare financial statements, household spending, account summaries, debt and guarantee documents, employee-plan information and relevant agreements. Ask which records are necessary and confirm a secure sharing method before providing sensitive documents.
