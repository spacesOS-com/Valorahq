Charitable giving: compare the gift route before assuming the tax result

"I want to give" can mean very different things on paper. You might put money into a donor-advised fund, give stock you own, or send a gift straight from an IRA. Each one has a different recipient, a different party in control, a different paper trail and a different tax question. This guide is a route comparison you can fill in before you talk to a custodian, a charity or a tax professional. It does not say which route is best for you, what you can deduct or how much tax a gift will change. It does not walk through any forms or transfers.

Key takeaways

  • The IRS says that once you contribute to a donor-advised fund, the sponsoring organization has legal control over it. You keep advisory privileges over how it is distributed and invested.
  • IRS Topic 506 says: "Gifts to individuals are not deductible. Only qualified organizations are eligible to receive tax deductible contributions."
  • The IRS says that for any contribution of $250 or more you need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the qualified organization.
  • Tax year matters. The IRS says that beginning in 2026 the rules for deducting charitable gifts changed in two ways: a limited deduction for cash gifts if you do not itemize, and a floor under itemized charitable deductions. A guide written for 2025 returns can be wrong for 2026.
  • A qualified charitable distribution (QCD) is a different route from a gift of cash or stock. The IRS describes it as a nontaxable distribution made directly by the trustee of your IRA to a qualified charity, and says you cannot also claim a charitable deduction for a QCD that is not included in your income.
  • A gift of stock is figured differently depending on how long you held it. The IRS says that if the property is worth more than you paid for it, you may have to reduce the deduction by the increase in value.

This guide is for US readers. It is educational, not individual tax, legal or investment advice. It does not work out any percentage limit for you, say whether any route can be combined with another, or cover estate gifts, business gifts or property other than stock. Three routes, kept apart

A donor-advised fund (DAF). The IRS says: "Generally, a donor advised fund is a separately identified fund or account that is maintained and operated by a section 501(c)(3) organization, which is called a sponsoring organization." It says: "Once the donor makes the contribution, the organization has legal control over it. However, the donor, or the donor's representative, retains advisory privileges with respect to the distribution of funds and the investment of assets in the account." Publication 526 (the edition for 2025 returns) says: "You can't deduct a contribution to a donor-advised fund if: ... You don't have a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from that sponsoring organization that it has exclusive legal control over the assets contributed." The same passage also lists a sponsor that is "a war veterans' organization, a fraternal society, or a nonprofit cemetery company," and adds: "There are also other circumstances in which you can't deduct your contribution to a donor-advised fund." The IRS page also says it is aware of organizations "promoted as donor-advised funds" that "appear to be established for the purpose of generating questionable charitable deductions." Ask who the sponsoring organization is.

A gift of stock or other property. IRS Topic 506 separates cash from other gifts: "In addition to deducting your cash contributions, you generally can deduct the fair market value of any other property you donate to qualified organizations." It points to Publication 561 for valuing noncash gifts. Publication 526 (the edition for 2025 returns) lists stocks among capital assets: "Examples of capital assets are stocks, bonds, jewelry, coin or stamp collections, and cars or furniture used for personal purposes." It also says: "If you contribute property with an FMV that is more than your basis in it, you may have to reduce the FMV by the amount of appreciation (increase in value) when you figure your deduction," and "Your basis in property is generally what you paid for it." It says the rules depend on whether the property is ordinary income property or capital gain property.

  • Ordinary income property. Publication 526 says: "Property is ordinary income property if you would have recognized ordinary income or short-term capital gain had you sold it at FMV on the date it was contributed." It names "capital assets ... held 1 year or less" as an example. For the deduction it says: "The amount you can deduct for a contribution of ordinary income property is its FMV minus the amount that would be ordinary income or short-term capital gain if you sold the property for its FMV. Generally, this rule limits the deduction to your basis in the property."
  • Capital gain property. Publication 526 says: "Property is capital gain property if you would have recognized long-term capital gain had you sold it at FMV on the date of the contribution. Capital gain property includes capital assets held more than 1 year." For the deduction it says: "When figuring your deduction for a contribution of capital gain property, you can generally use the FMV of the property." It then lists situations where you must reduce the FMV, including gifts "to certain private nonoperating foundations," and says of those foundation gifts: "the reduced deduction doesn't apply to contributions of qualified appreciated stock." It defines that as "any stock in a corporation that is capital gain property and for which market quotations are readily available on an established securities market on the day of the contribution," except "to the extent you and your family contributed more than 10% of the value of all the outstanding stock in the corporation."
  • Percentage limits. Publication 526 says: "Your deduction for charitable contributions generally can't be more than 60% of your AGI, but in some cases 20%, 30%, or 50% limits may apply." For capital gain property it says: "Your noncash contributions of capital gain property to 50% limit organizations is limited to 30% of your AGI minus all your contributions to 50% limit organizations that are subject to the 60% and 50% limits (other than qualified conservation contributions)." It says a 20% limit applies when you make "noncash contributions of capital gain property during the year (1) to an organization described earlier under Second category of qualified organizations, or (2) 'for the use of' any qualified organization." It also describes an election: "You may choose the 50% limit for contributions of capital gain property to qualified organizations described earlier under First category of qualified organizations (50% limit organizations) instead of the 30% limit that would otherwise apply. If you make this choice, you must reduce the FMV of the property contributed by the appreciation in value that would have been long-term capital gain if the property had been sold." It adds: "You must make the choice on your original return or on an amended return filed by the due date for filing the original return." This guide does not say which category an organization falls into. Ask the organization and a tax professional.

IRS Topic 506 says: "You must fill out one or more Forms 8283, Noncash Charitable Contributions and attach them to your return, if your deduction for any noncash contribution is more than $500." For more than $5,000 per item or group of similar items it says you "must obtain a qualified appraisal." Whether any of this changes for 2026 is a question for a tax professional. Publication 526 is the 2025 edition.

An IRA qualified charitable distribution (QCD). Publication 590-B (the edition for 2025 returns) says: "A QCD is generally a nontaxable distribution made directly by the trustee of your IRA (other than an ongoing SEP or SIMPLE IRA) to an organization eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions. You must be at least age 70½ when the distribution is made. Also, you must have the same type of acknowledgment of your contribution that you would need to claim a deduction for a charitable contribution." It also says:

  • "The maximum annual exclusion for QCDs is $108,000. Any QCD in excess of the $108,000 exclusion limit is included in income as any other distribution. If you file a joint return, your spouse can also have a QCD and exclude up to $108,000." That is the figure in the 2025 edition. The limit can change from year to year, so check the current IRS figure before you rely on any number.
  • "The amount of the QCD is limited to the amount of the distribution that would otherwise be included in income. If your IRA includes nondeductible contributions, the distribution is first considered to be paid out of otherwise taxable income."
  • "You can't claim a charitable contribution deduction for any QCD not included in your income."
  • "A QCD will count towards your required minimum distribution."
  • "Beginning in tax years after December 31, 2019, the amount of QCDs that you can exclude from income is reduced by the excess of the aggregate amount of IRA contributions you deducted for the taxable year and any prior year that you were age 70½ or older over the amount of the IRA contributions that were used to reduce the excludable amount of QCDs in all earlier years."

Do not mix up giving stock to a donor-advised fund and making a QCD to one. They are different routes. Publication 590-B describes a QCD as going to an organization eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions and does not address donor-advised funds in the passage this guide uses, so this guide does not say whether a QCD can go to one. Ask your IRA custodian and your tax professional before you assume.

What changed for 2026

IRS Topic 506 says: "Beginning with tax year 2026, if you do not itemize, you may deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 if filing jointly) of your cash contributions to certain qualified organizations." Publication 505 (2026) says: "Beginning in 2026, you can claim a deduction for cash contributions made to eligible tax-exempt organizations. You don't have to itemize to take the deduction. The maximum deduction is $1,000 ($2,000 for married filing jointly) with certain other limitations." Publication 505 (2026) also says: "Beginning in 2026, if you itemize, you can only deduct charitable contributions that are more than 0.5% of your adjusted gross income. Any amount that falls under the 0.5% floor can't be deducted in 2026." Both describe the non-itemizer deduction as being for cash contributions. This guide does not say whether it applies to a gift to a donor-advised fund.

Publication 526 on the IRS site is the edition for 2025 returns. Do not use it as the source for 2026 percentages, thresholds or exceptions without checking the current IRS pages.

Records the IRS says to keep

The IRS says that for any cash, check or other monetary gift, you must keep a bank record or a written communication from the qualified organization with its name, the amount and the date. For any contribution of $250 or more, you must obtain a contemporaneous written acknowledgment that gives the amount of cash and a description of any property other than cash, and says whether the organization provided goods or services in exchange. If you receive a benefit in exchange, such as admission to a banquet, Topic 506 says "you can only deduct the amount that exceeds the fair market value of the benefit received or expected to be received." These are rules the IRS describes. Valora does not file forms or arrange transfers.

Questions to bring to a professional

  • Which route fits what I actually want to give, and what do my own facts change about the tax result?
  • If I give stock, how long have I held it, what did I pay for it, and which limit applies to the organization receiving it?
  • Which tax year will each gift fall in, and which 2026 rules apply to it?
  • Is the organization I want to support a qualified organization, and who is the sponsoring organization if I use a donor-advised fund?
  • If I am 70½ or older with an IRA, how would a QCD and my required distribution fit together, and who at my custodian handles it?
  • Who looks at the whole picture, including taxes and investments? See Valora's guide on managing investments and ask a professional about your own facts.

Documents to gather

  • Written acknowledgments or bank records from organizations you have given to this year.
  • Statements for the account the gift would come from, kept private.
  • For a donor-advised fund, the sponsor's acknowledgment of legal control.
  • For an IRA, your custodian's description of how it handles direct payments to charities.
  • A copy of the comparison above.

Bottom line

A donor-advised fund, a gift of stock and an IRA QCD are three different routes with different recipients, control, records and tax questions. The IRS has changed the rules for 2026, so check the year. For each route, write down who ends up with legal control, what paperwork you need, which tax question is open and who can answer it. The comparison does not choose a route for you. It makes the next conversation shorter.

Common questions

Who controls money I put in a donor-advised fund?

The IRS says the sponsoring organization has legal control once you contribute. You, or your representative, keep advisory privileges over distributions and investments.

Can I deduct a charitable gift if I do not itemize?

The IRS says that beginning in tax year 2026, you may deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 if filing jointly) of cash contributions to certain qualified organizations if you do not itemize, with certain other limitations. Ask a tax professional about your own situation.

What is a qualified charitable distribution?

The IRS describes it as an otherwise taxable distribution from an IRA, other than an ongoing SEP or SIMPLE IRA, owned by someone 70½ or older, paid directly from the IRA to a qualified charity. It can count toward a required minimum distribution. Publication 590-B (2025 edition) also says you cannot claim a charitable deduction for a QCD that is not included in your income.

What records does the IRS say I need?

A bank record or written communication from the organization for any monetary gift, and a contemporaneous written acknowledgment for any contribution of $250 or more.
