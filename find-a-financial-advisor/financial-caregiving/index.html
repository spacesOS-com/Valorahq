Helping a parent or relative with money: what to clarify first

If you are starting to help a parent or relative with money, the first questions are about your role, not their accounts. What are you being asked to do? Is there a legal document, or is this informal help? Who else knows? This guide uses the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's (CFPB's) Managing Someone Else's Money materials to separate informal help from formal roles, then gives you a worksheet of questions to settle and questions to bring to a professional. It does not decide whether anyone is able to manage their own money, and it does not say what your state's law allows.

Key takeaways

  • The CFPB describes informal help as helping on an as-needed basis. It says if more than occasional help is needed, it might be time to name a formal caregiver.
  • The CFPB lists these formal roles: power of attorney, guardian or conservator, trustee, and VA fiduciary or Social Security representative payee. Each has a different source of authority.
  • The CFPB says an agent under a power of attorney has authority strictly limited to what the document and state law allow.
  • The CFPB says a convenience account does not change who owns the money, and that a trusted contact person does not have access to the money.
  • State laws vary. A lawyer in your state can tell you what applies.

This guide is for US readers. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time.

Informal help and formal roles are different things

The CFPB's "Considering a financial caregiver? Know your options" guide (March 2021) lays out two groups.

Informal help, as the CFPB describes it:

  • Conversation partner. A trusted relative, friend or professional gets an overview of someone's finances, even if not all the details are shared. The CFPB's examples include asking a banker to send a copy of statements to a daughter or accountant, or having a trusted person join a visit to the bank.
  • Trusted contact person. Brokerage accounts, and some banks, can add one. The CFPB says it allows the institution to contact that person in certain circumstances, like if it believes the account holder is being scammed, and that trusted contacts do not have access to the money.
  • Convenience account. The CFPB says it lets you name someone to help deposit, withdraw and write checks. It says this is not a joint account, does not change who owns the money and does not give the helper the right to keep it when the owner dies.

Formal roles, as the CFPB describes them:

  • Power of attorney. A legal document that gives someone legal authority to make decisions about money or property. The CFPB says the authority of an agent ends when the principal dies. A health care power of attorney is for health care decisions, not money.
  • Guardian or conservator. The CFPB lists court-appointed guardians and conservators as a formal role for managing money and property. Whether an appointment is available or needed depends on the court, the documents and state law.
  • Trustee. The CFPB says a trustee manages only the money or property held in the trust, not any other assets.
  • VA fiduciary or Social Security representative payee. The CFPB says the agency may appoint someone to manage that agency's benefits only, and that other property or benefits need one of the other formal arrangements.

Which of these applies, or whether any does, depends on documents and state law. This guide does not tell you which one your situation needs.

Clarify these before you start

Use this as a conversation guide, not a legal checklist.

  1. What is being asked of you? Paying a bill once, watching for scams, managing accounts regularly, or something larger? The CFPB says that trusting someone with your finances, even informally, is an important decision with a lot of responsibility attached.
  2. Is there a document? If someone named you in a power of attorney, trust or court order, ask to read it. The CFPB's guide for agents says your authority is strictly limited to what the document and state law allow, and to follow the principal's directions in it.
  3. What do the institutions say? The CFPB says to ask a banker or broker what informal options they offer. Do not assume a bank will recognize a role you describe. The CFPB's guide for agents says others may not understand what an agent is or does and may think you have more or less authority than you do.
  4. What does the person you are helping want shared? The CFPB says a caregiver should include the person in decisions as much as possible.
  5. Who else is involved? The CFPB's guide for agents says family and friends may disagree with decisions, and that sharing summaries of how money was spent can help unless the principal said not to.
  6. Is there a back-up? The CFPB's options guide mentions choosing a back-up, called a successor fiduciary, and says to review the choice every so often.

Records and money handling

The CFPB's guide for agents under a power of attorney lists four basic duties. These duties describe agents under a power of attorney. Other roles have different authority and duties.

  • Act only in the person's best interest. The CFPB says to avoid conflicts of interest.
  • Manage the person's money and property carefully.
  • Keep the person's money and property separate from yours. The CFPB says never to deposit it into your own or someone else's account, to avoid joint accounts and to get legal advice before changing one that already exists, and to sign as the agent rather than as the person.
  • Keep good records. The CFPB says to keep a detailed list of everything received or spent, with dates, amounts, reasons and names, and to keep receipts and notes even for small expenses.

These are the CFPB's descriptions for agents. If you are not an agent under a power of attorney, ask a lawyer how they apply to you.

A worksheet: what to settle and who to ask

Fill in your own details. Do not write account numbers, passwords or Social Security numbers on it.

Question My answer so far Who can confirm it
What am I being asked to do, in plain words? The person I am helping
Is there a power of attorney, trust or court order? Where is it? The person, or their lawyer
What has each bank or institution said it will accept from me? Each institution
What records will I keep, and where? Me, then a lawyer
Who else in the family knows about this arrangement? The person I am helping
What happens if I can no longer help? The person, and a lawyer

Questions for professionals

  • Does the document I hold give me authority for what I am being asked to do, and does state law change that?
  • If I am helping informally, what do I need to be careful about?
  • Should the person I am helping consider a different arrangement?
  • How should I keep records, and who should see them?
  • Which of these questions is for a lawyer, a banker, an accountant or a financial advisor?

The CFPB's options guide suggests contacting an elder law attorney, local legal aid or the Eldercare Locator when you need legal help or do not have someone to ask.

Documents to gather

  • Any signed power of attorney, trust, court order or agency appointment, with its date.
  • A list of institutions the person uses, without account numbers on the list.
  • Recent statements the person agrees to share.
  • Your own record-keeping log from the first day.

Bottom line

Before helping a parent or relative with money, clarify what you are being asked to do, whether a legal document exists and what it says, what each institution will accept, what records you will keep and who else knows. The CFPB separates informal help from formal roles because they carry different authority. A lawyer in your state can tell you what applies.

Common questions

What should I clarify before helping a parent or relative with money?

Your role, whether a legal document exists, what each institution accepts, what records you will keep and who else knows. See the worksheet above.

Is helping informally the same as having power of attorney?

No. The CFPB describes informal options, such as a convenience account or trusted contact person, as separate from a power of attorney. It says a convenience account does not change ownership of the money.

Does this guide say whether my parent can manage their own money?

No. It does not make capacity determinations. A physician or lawyer would speak to that.

Does this guide say what my state allows?

No. State laws vary. Ask a lawyer in your state.
