Estate planning: organize the documents and questions before you meet a lawyer

Meeting a lawyer about estate planning goes better when you know which documents you already have, where they are and what you want to ask. This guide helps you prepare for that meeting. It gives you a worksheet in three groups: your finances during life, your health care preferences and what happens to property after death. It does not draft a will or trust, does not tell you what you need, and does not decide who owns what or whether any document is valid. A lawyer in your state does that.

Key takeaways

  • The American Bar Association (ABA) describes estate planning as a process that involves professional advisors familiar with your goals, your assets, how they are owned and your family structure. It names lawyers, accountants, financial planners, life insurance advisors, bankers and brokers as examples.
  • The National Institute on Aging (NIA) suggests keeping important papers and copies of legal documents in one place and telling someone you trust, or a lawyer, where to find them.
  • A financial power of attorney and a health care power of attorney are different documents. The NIA describes them separately.
  • The ABA says a will does not govern assets that pass by beneficiary designation or title, and calls reviewing beneficiary designations a critical part of the process.
  • The CFPB says a power of attorney gives someone a great deal of authority over your finances without regular oversight, and that working with a lawyer could help protect you against abuse.

This guide is for US readers. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. The ABA notes that state laws on intestacy and powers of attorney vary, so this guide does not give state-specific rules, tax thresholds or document templates.

Three groups of documents

The NIA's checklist separates planning for your estate and finances from planning for future health care. This guide uses three groups so the questions do not get mixed up.

During life: financial authority. The NIA describes a durable power of attorney for finances as naming someone to make financial decisions for you when you are unable to. The CFPB says a power of attorney is a legal document that allows someone else to act on your behalf, and that the person is called an agent. A living trust, as the NIA describes it, names a trustee to hold and distribute property and funds on your behalf when you are no longer able to manage your affairs.

Health care preferences. The NIA describes advance directives as legal documents with instructions for medical care that take effect only if you cannot communicate your own wishes. The most common are a living will, which tells doctors how you want to be treated, and a durable power of attorney for health care, which names a health care proxy. The NIA lists a financial power of attorney and a health care power of attorney as separate documents.

After death: property instructions. The NIA says a will specifies how your estate will be distributed and managed when you die, and that if you do not have one, your estate will be distributed according to your state's laws. The ABA says a will provides for property you own at death, and does not govern property passing by beneficiary designation or title, such as property owned jointly with survivorship rights, payable-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts. A will is not a substitute for checking those arrangements.

A trust is not a will, and a trust holds only what is in it

The CFPB describes a revocable living trust as an arrangement created through a legal document that gives someone the power to make decisions about money or property held in the trust. It names three roles: the person who makes the trust, the trustee and the beneficiaries. The CFPB says the trust is ineffective until the person who makes it puts money or property into it, and that a trustee has no legal authority over money or property that is not in the trust. People use trusts for several reasons, the CFPB says, including keeping control of property and naming who receives it after death. Whether a trust fits your situation is a question for a lawyer. This guide does not explain how to set one up or fund one.

Questions to bring to the lawyer

  • For each group, which documents does my situation call for, and which does it not?
  • When and how does each document take effect, and what does my state require?
  • If I have a power of attorney, how can I limit it, or require my agent to report to someone? The CFPB says you can require reports to another person.
  • The CFPB lists ways a power of attorney can be abused, including an agent who spends your money on themself or does things you did not authorize, such as making gifts or changing beneficiaries. What can the document say about those?
  • If I have beneficiary designations, how do they fit with my will?
  • If I have a trust, which property is actually in it?
  • How often should these documents be reviewed? The NIA suggests reviewing plans at least once a year and when a major life event occurs, such as a divorce, move or major change in health.

Keep the papers findable

The NIA suggests putting your papers and copies of legal documents in one place, such as a file, a drawer or a notebook that lists information and locations. If papers are in a bank safe deposit box, it says to keep copies in a file at home. It also says someone you trust should know where to find your papers in an emergency, and that you do not need to discuss your personal affairs to do that. If you have no one you trust, the NIA says to ask a lawyer to help. Valora's guide on disaster financial records covers backup copies and access.

Documents to gather

  • The names and locations of the documents in the three groups, not their contents.
  • A list of the professionals you already work with.
  • Your questions from the worksheet.

Bottom line

Before you meet a lawyer, list your documents in three groups, note where each current version is, write your questions and mark each one open. The NIA lists a financial power of attorney and a health care power of attorney as separate documents, and the ABA says a will does not govern property that passes by beneficiary designation or title, so list each one on its own row. The lawyer, not this guide, decides what applies to you.

Common questions

What should I gather before an estate-planning meeting with a lawyer?

A list of the documents you have in three groups, where each current version is kept and your questions. See the worksheet above.

Is a financial power of attorney the same as a health care power of attorney?

No. The NIA describes them as separate documents with separate purposes.

Does a will cover everything I own?

The ABA says no. A will does not govern property that passes by beneficiary designation or title, such as retirement accounts, life insurance and jointly owned property with survivorship rights.

Does this guide tell me what documents I need?

No. A lawyer in your state can say what applies to you.

What should I send through the inquiry form?

Describe the question you want to ask. Do not submit bank statements, account numbers, wills, trust documents or other confidential documents.
