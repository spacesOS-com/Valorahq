After a job loss: which records to update and which questions to settle first

Losing a job starts several clocks at once: an unemployment claim, a coverage decision, and the tax treatment of whatever pay continues. Each belongs to a different office, and the order you work them in affects what you receive and when. File the claim first, put every coverage date in writing, treat severance as a document to review rather than a number to spend, and write down the questions that belong to a plan contact or tax professional before any review.

Key takeaways

  • File the unemployment claim as soon as possible; first payments generally take two to three weeks.
  • Severance pay is taxable income - review the agreement's terms and dates before signing anything.
  • You usually have 60 days to elect COBRA, and a job loss also opens a 60-day Marketplace window; compare the routes before choosing.
  • Unemployment compensation is taxable too; you can ask to have federal tax withheld from it.

This guide is for US readers organizing the paperwork side of a layoff or other involuntary job loss. It organizes records and questions; it does not recommend a coverage route, interpret a severance agreement, or advise on any account. File for unemployment first

Unemployment insurance is a joint state-federal program, and each state runs its own program under federal guidelines. The Department of Labor says to file your claim with the state where you worked, as soon as possible after becoming unemployed, and that it generally takes two to three weeks after filing to receive a first benefit payment. Eligibility usually requires being unemployed through no fault of your own and meeting the state's work and wage requirements over a base period. [1]

Two details keep claims moving: file in the right state - if you worked in a different state than you live in, or in several states, your current state's agency can explain how to file - and give complete and correct information about your former employment, because gaps delay the claim. [1] The filing date starts everything else, so treat it as the first task, not the backup plan.

Treat severance as a document, not a number

If your employer offers severance, the agreement is a legal document with terms worth reading slowly: the amount and payment schedule, whether any benefits continue and for how long, what you agree to in exchange, and any deadline for signing. This page does not interpret agreements; those terms are questions for a professional reviewing your actual document.

One fact is settled and worth knowing before you plan around the money: severance is taxable pay. IRS Publication 525 says you must include severance pay in income, that it is subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes and income tax withholding, and that if you accept reduced severance in exchange for outplacement services, you still include the unreduced amount. [6] Plan with the after-tax figure, or mark it as an open question.

Pick a coverage route by its dates

A job loss usually offers two routes to staying covered, and each has its own clock.

COBRA lets you keep the job-based coverage you had. The Department of Labor says you generally pay the full premium yourself unless the employer agrees to cover some of it, that spouses and children can enroll even if you do not, and that you have 60 days to enroll, starting from when your job-based coverage ends or when you receive the COBRA election notice, whichever is later. [3]

The Marketplace is the parallel route. HealthCare.gov says losing job-based coverage in the past 60 days - or expecting to lose it in the next 60 days - may qualify you for a Special Enrollment Period. [4] Compare the routes by premium, doctors, deductible progress so far this year and the actual decision dates, and record each date from the plan or the Marketplace itself, not from a general article.

Plan the tax side early

Unemployment compensation is taxable income. The IRS says you generally must include all of it, that you can choose to have federal income tax withheld by filing Form W-4V, that you may otherwise need to make quarterly estimated payments, and that you will receive a Form 1099-G showing the year's benefits and any withholding. [2]

The rest of the year's withholding picture changes too. When income drops mid-year, the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator can rerun the numbers from your most recent pay stubs - it takes about 25 minutes, saves no personal information and produces a pre-filled Form W-4 for any remaining or new job. The IRS lists a major income change among the events that should trigger a withholding check. [5]

Price the runway as a household question

The money side of a job loss is a runway question: how many months do the incoming amounts cover the outgoing ones. The incoming side is severance after tax, unemployment benefits after any withholding, and any savings you choose to count. The outgoing side is the real monthly spend, plus any new coverage premium once the old one ends.

Write both sides down from actual documents - the agreement, the state's benefit determination, the plan's premium notice - rather than estimates from memory. Compare the totals month by month and note where the gap is largest and when the first hard date lands, whether that is a COBRA election deadline or the month savings run short. Those dates are the ones a review should focus on. This page does not recommend a budget, a coverage route or a drawdown order; it organizes the questions so they arrive in one place, early enough to matter.

Use a private job-loss worksheet

Copy these six fields into private notes. The worksheet organizes evidence; it is not a tax filing aid, a benefits election, a legal review or a coverage application.

1. Unemployment claim

[blank] State filed in, date filed, and the date the determination or first payment arrives.

2. Severance review

[blank] Amount and schedule, benefits continuation, what is agreed to in exchange, and the signing deadline - with questions written for the professional reviewing the document.

3. Coverage route and date

[blank] COBRA election or Marketplace enrollment; the exact deadline as the plan or Marketplace confirms it.

4. Coverage cost change

[blank] The full monthly premium under each route and the date the old premium stops.

5. Tax withholding choices

[blank] Whether Form W-4V was filed for unemployment, and the date the estimator was rerun for any remaining income.

6. Runway months

[blank] Monthly incoming and outgoing totals from documents, with the first month the gap appears.

A job loss often arrives alongside other changes, and each has its own page here. Common questions

How soon should I file for unemployment?

As soon as possible after becoming unemployed. The Department of Labor says to file with the state where you worked and that it generally takes two to three weeks after filing to receive a first payment, so the filing date sets the timeline. [1]

Is severance pay taxable?

Yes. IRS Publication 525 says severance pay must be included in income and is subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes and income tax withholding, and that accepting reduced severance in exchange for outplacement services still leaves the unreduced amount taxable. [6]

How long do I have to decide about COBRA?

The Department of Labor says you have 60 days to enroll, starting when your job-based coverage ends or when you receive the COBRA election notice, whichever is later. You generally pay the full premium. Losing job-based coverage also opens a 60-day Marketplace Special Enrollment Period, so compare the routes before the earlier date. [3] [4]

Do I owe taxes on unemployment benefits?

Generally yes. The IRS says unemployment compensation is included in income; you can request federal withholding with Form W-4V or make estimated payments, and you will receive a Form 1099-G for the year. [2]

What records does this page not cover?

This page does not cover retirement account decisions after leaving an employer, negotiating a severance agreement, or wrongful-termination questions. Each is a separate reviewed question with its own documents and, for some, its own professional.
