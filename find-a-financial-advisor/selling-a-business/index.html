Selling a business: choose the work before accepting terms

Choose professional help around the decisions in the proposed deal, not around an assumed investment payout. Have legal and tax specialists check the transaction while a clearly scoped planning engagement connects uncertain proceeds and obligations to your household goals.

This guide is for US owners considering a sale, not owners already investing the proceeds. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. This page offers no specialist roster, service availability or promised result.

Key takeaways

  • Define the legal, tax and household-planning work before accepting sale terms, and assign each task to a qualified professional.
  • Separate headline consideration from closing cash, conditional proceeds, retained equity and obligations before estimating household availability.
  • Have the actual transaction reviewed for tax treatment and timing rather than applying one capital-gains rate to the price.
  • Compare written scope, total costs and incentives before committing future sale proceeds to investment management.

Before accepting terms, assign the jobs

Choose a transaction attorney and tax professional for the agreement and tax questions, then define the financial-planning work around them. An investment-management engagement is not a substitute for either professional's review of the sale.

The SBA sale-of-business guide recommends valuation before marketing, accurate identification of property being sold and attorney review of the sales agreement. It calls out adjustments, broker fees, assets and liabilities. Use that as a starting agenda, not proof that one professional can handle every transaction. Depending on the business, the team may also need a valuation specialist, transaction intermediary or other specialist. Ask what each person is qualified and engaged to do.

Give each task a named owner: interpreting legal obligations, checking tax assumptions, reconciling expected proceeds and modeling the household decision. Ask who updates the others when a term changes. A meeting where everyone assumes someone else checked the same number leaves you with a polished estimate and an unowned risk.

Define the decision before hiring a portfolio manager

Write the pre-deal question you need answered before comparing advisers. It might be whether the sale could support a change in work, what cash needs attention before closing or how uncertain proceeds affect the household plan.

Ask for a planning deliverable built around that question: a document inventory, proceeds bridge, scenario comparison and list of assumptions awaiting counsel or tax review. This is a proposed engagement brief, not a claim that every adviser offers that service. Investor.gov explains that services and fees depend on the negotiated contract and that advisers may provide financial planning as well as investment advice.

Compare a bounded pre-sale assignment with an ongoing engagement. What is due before the next decision? What continues after closing? Do you have to move assets to receive planning work? If an adviser discusses only how they will invest an expected payout, bring the conversation back to the terms, uncertainties and household question that exist today.

Separate the headline price from cash you can use

A headline price is not a household spending balance. Ask the team to reconcile what is quoted with what would be received at closing, what remains conditional and what obligations would still need funding.

Start by identifying what the price describes. Is it for company assets, an ownership interest or another defined transaction amount? Are debt, retained assets or adjustments already included? Do not subtract the same debt or fee twice because two presentations use different starting points. Request the definition beside each number and trace it to the relevant draft term or schedule.

Put escrow, deferred payments, earnouts and retained equity in separate lines. An estimate of future value is not money received today. Ask counsel to explain the conditions for release or payment and the rights or restrictions on retained ownership. Ask the planning professional what happens if a conditional amount is delayed or never received, rather than treating the buyer's maximum illustration as a certainty.

The SBA guide emphasizes recording adjustments and relevant terms in the agreement. The worksheet below turns those questions into an organizing aid. It is not a valuation, closing statement or tax calculation.

Talk it through

Have tax treatment reviewed asset by asset

Do not estimate every business sale by multiplying the headline price by a capital-gains rate. Ask a tax professional what is being sold, the relevant basis and classifications, the seller's circumstances and which assumptions remain unsettled.

The IRS sale-of-a-business guidance explains that a business asset sale is generally treated as separate sales of its assets. Inventory can produce ordinary income or loss, while other assets have different treatment. The same page distinguishes partnership interests and corporate stock and discusses allocation of consideration in applicable asset transfers. These distinctions are reasons to request review, not a recommendation for one transaction structure.

Ask how a proposed allocation connects to the agreement, records and expected reporting. What information is missing? What changes if consideration or terms change? Federal treatment is not a complete state-tax or personal-tax answer. Entity and owner consequences may need separate analysis.

This guide does not prescribe qualified small business stock treatment, a legal structure or a tax election. It does not use a simple investment-gains calculator as a business-sale model. A useful estimate should show its documents, assumptions and reviewer, rather than presenting one precise after-tax number without support.

Review timing as well as total consideration

Deferred or conditional proceeds need their own tax and legal review. Payment timing and tax recognition are not interchangeable, so do not assume taxes wait until every dollar reaches your account.

IRS Publication 537 explains installment-sale treatment and its exceptions. Inventory does not qualify for that treatment, and depreciation recapture can be reportable in the year of sale even without receiving an installment payment that year. It also addresses contingent payment sales and escrow arrangements. This is background for questions, not a conclusion that your deal qualifies.

Ask for a cash-and-obligation timeline with uncertain items marked separately. Which amounts are known, estimated or conditional? Who checks tax payment assumptions against proposed terms? Who explains what an escrow restriction or deferred promise means legally? A household plan should not spend an expected payment before its conditions and risks are understood.

Stress-test the household question, not a sale multiple

Compare what your household would need under more than one plausible transaction outcome. Ask how the plan changes if closing is delayed, conditional proceeds are lower or you continue working longer than expected.

List regular spending, near-term goals, debt commitments, existing liquid resources and income that could continue or end. These are discussion inputs, not a recommended reserve or allocation. Separate goals requiring cash soon from goals that may tolerate a longer horizon. Ask what information would change the conclusion and which decision can wait until the agreement is firmer.

Keep business obligations distinct from personal ones. A sale may leave questions about guarantees, employment arrangements, insurance, leases or retained ownership. Have the relevant professional identify what applies rather than importing a generic exit checklist. The planning professional should show how confirmed obligations enter the household picture without interpreting legal clauses outside their scope.

Do not use an invented industry multiple to justify the plan. Valuation and household planning answer different questions. Ask for a sensitivity comparison based on terms under discussion, not a prediction of what another buyer will pay.

Compare scope, fees and incentives before committing

Ask candidates to put work, exclusions and compensation in writing. Compare the cost of pre-deal planning separately from investment-management costs that might begin after closing.

Investor.gov explains Form CRS as a way to understand services, fees, authority, conflicts and disciplinary information for applicable firms serving retail investors. Read the engagement agreement and Form ADV brochure where applicable. Ask how fees change if the transaction does not close, proceeds are smaller or you do not hire the adviser for ongoing management.

An adviser expecting to manage sale proceeds may have a financial incentive connected to assets moving into their management. Ask about the actual arrangement rather than assuming a fee label eliminates conflicts. Does someone receive referral compensation? Are recommended products affiliated with the firm? What work can you buy without committing future assets?

Check the firm and individual through official registration resources, then separately ask for relevant experience and a redacted sample deliverable. Registration is not a business-sale expertise endorsement. A superficially similar industry example does not establish that the person can review your transaction.

A fictional case to test the process

Consider a fictional owner offered closing cash, a possible earnout and retained equity. The first assignment is to identify what each component means and who must verify it, not recommend investments for the combined headline figure.

Counsel reviews payment and ownership provisions within the engagement. The tax professional evaluates transaction facts and reporting questions. The planner models the household decision using checked assumptions and separates money expected soon from money that may never become available. These are suggested responsibilities to confirm, not a real client story or promised outcome.

Ask a candidate what they need before telling this owner the sale supports a work change. If the answer jumps to a portfolio, ask which proceeds and obligations were assumed. If it promises tax savings before seeing documents, ask who confirmed treatment. The test is whether the process exposes missing information.

Prepare the first meeting without oversharing

Bring questions and document types before sharing confidential deal materials. Verify the professional, agree on scope and use an agreed secure method when records are needed; do not upload agreements or financial statements to a public inquiry form.

The list can name the draft agreement, ownership records, balance sheet, debt schedule, prior tax information and household goals without sending everything on the first call. Ask which documents each professional needs, why and how access is controlled. Counsel may need to guide disclosure of confidential transaction information.

Ask: What is the first deliverable? Which assumptions will you verify? Who checks tax and legal work? What stays outside your scope? How do fees change if the deal changes? Who coordinates updates before I accept terms? Write down owners and the next question.

For broader ongoing planning, read the business-owner educational guide. The owner advisor-selection guide covers broader scope comparison. After closing, the assignment shifts to received cash, signed documents and remaining commitments rather than repeating pre-deal negotiation work.

Conclusion: choose the work before choosing the portfolio

Before accepting sale terms, have the right professionals check the transaction and connect verified assumptions to the household decision. Headline price, closing cash and conditional proceeds are different inputs, not one spendable balance.

Use the checklist privately, name the professional responsible for each open question and compare written planning scopes before committing future assets. Resolve the terms and tax questions first; an investment proposal cannot answer them by itself.

Common questions

Do I need a financial advisor before selling my business?

A defined planning assignment may help connect proposed proceeds and obligations to household goals. It does not replace a transaction attorney or tax professional. Ask what would be delivered before the next decision, which facts need review and whether you can buy planning without committing future assets.

Why is the headline price different from available cash?

The price may include amounts not received at closing, retained equity or conditional payments. Debt, fees, adjustments, taxes and remaining commitments may also affect the picture. Reconcile definitions and documents first so you do not count the same deduction twice.

Is all gain from a business sale taxed as capital gain?

No. IRS sale-of-business guidance explains that asset sales can involve different treatment for different assets, including ordinary income or loss for inventory. Ask a tax professional to review the actual transaction, seller and asset classifications rather than apply one rate to the headline price.

Can I assume taxes on deferred proceeds wait until payment?

No. IRS Publication 537 describes installment-sale conditions and exceptions, including inventory and depreciation recapture. Escrow and contingent payments also need fact-specific review. Ask a tax professional to compare payment timing with recognition and payment obligations.

What should I ask about the advisor's fees before the sale?

Ask for the planning fee, exclusions, costs if the transaction changes or fails, and any later management fees. Ask whether compensation changes when proceeds move into management and whether there are referral or affiliated-product incentives. Compare the agreement and applicable disclosures.

Does this page value my business or arrange an introduction?

No. The worksheet organizes questions; it does not calculate valuation, tax or a suitable portfolio. Valora is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. Verify any professional's scope, experience and disclosures independently.
