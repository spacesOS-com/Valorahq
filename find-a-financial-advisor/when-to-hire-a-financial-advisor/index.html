Guide

When is it time to hire a financial advisor?

Consider hiring a financial advisor when you can name a decision you need help with, explain what you cannot settle yourself and compare a written service proposal against that need. An approaching deadline, several connected decisions or a need for ongoing review can be reasons to explore paid help. None creates a universal rule that you must hire someone.

Start with the work, not a wealth threshold. A household can have a specific planning question without wanting investment management. Another household may want someone to review investments regularly. Those are different assignments, with different costs and responsibilities.

Key takeaways

  • Write the decision and deadline before looking for a professional.
  • Separate a one-time question from ongoing management.
  • Compare written scope, costs and exclusions against the same need.
  • Check the professional's background before sharing private records.

Name the decision that is still open

Describe the question in one sentence. Perhaps you want to understand the records behind a retirement decision, compare competing proposals or organize several household changes into a review. Keep the question specific enough that someone can explain what they would deliver.

Write what you already know and what is missing. A document question may need an employer or plan contact before a planning assignment makes sense. A tax filing or legal document may call for a separate professional. Do not assume that a financial advisor's proposal includes every kind of work.

An income change by itself does not define the service. If your immediate question is payment evidence and bill timing, the household income change guide offers a private worksheet. If the question concerns a new job, the new-job guide focuses on offer, payroll and plan documents. Use those questions to describe the work you want, not to decide in advance that you need an ongoing account relationship.

Decide whether the work is one-time or ongoing

A useful proposal explains whether you receive one written comparison, a defined planning project or continuing service. Ask what triggers another review, who is responsible for updates and what is excluded. "We help with retirement" does not tell you what happens after the first conversation.

The SEC's Investor.gov relationship-summary guide explains how Form CRS describes a firm's services, fees, conflicts and other information. Read the relevant firm's documents rather than treating all people called financial advisors as offering the same work. Ask whether the firm can answer your particular question without requiring services you did not seek.

You can explore a proposal without committing to move money or open an account. Do not mistake a sales presentation or a calculator output for a written assignment. A recommendation to change investments is a separate decision from whether to pay for a review.

Talk it through

Use a private readiness worksheet

Keep six fields: the decision; the date it matters; the records already available; the missing answer or dependency; the work product you want; and the cost, exclusions and follow-up proposed. Keep personal account details out of a public inquiry.

For each possible service, record what would change after the work is done. Would you understand a comparison, receive a written plan or delegate specified ongoing tasks? If that remains unclear, ask the professional to narrow the scope before paying for something broad.

The worksheet is an original preparation aid. It does not rate your financial health or tell you whether a proposed strategy is suitable. There is no score or dollar cutoff at which the worksheet says you must hire someone.

Compare the costs in dollars and the responsibilities in writing

The SEC's guide to working with an investment professional encourages asking how the professional is paid and translating percentage fees into dollars. Ask for the proposed cost, when it is charged and which other charges are outside it. Do not choose on a headline percentage alone.

The FINRA guide to working with an investment professional recommends defining your needs, discussing fees and asking about additional compensation. Use those questions alongside the exact service proposal. Different compensation arrangements do not automatically mean the same service or total cost.

Compare proposals against one question and one set of assumptions. Ask what you must do yourself, how private documents will be exchanged and what happens if you end the relationship. Keep unanswered points visible rather than assuming a reassuring introduction settles them.

Check the person and firm before sharing records

The SEC's Ask and Check guide points to public registration and background resources. Use the relevant official record and check that the identity and firm match the proposal. A familiar title or website biography is not a substitute for that check.

If you cannot tell which service is offered, how it is paid or who will do the work, you have a question to resolve before committing. You may decide to gather missing documents first, ask for a narrower proposal or continue handling a defined task yourself. This guide does not decide that choice for you.

Bring a clear question to the first conversation

It may be time to explore professional help when a specific unresolved decision needs a kind of review you want to pay for. The next step is to compare scope, costs and responsibilities, not to assume that hiring guarantees an outcome. Common questions

Is there a savings amount at which everyone needs an advisor?

This guide sets no universal savings threshold. Start with the decision, the work you want and the cost and scope of the proposed service.

Does a life change mean I need ongoing investment management?

Not automatically. A document question, one-time planning project and ongoing management are different assignments.

What should I prepare before a first conversation?

Prepare the decision, deadline, available records, missing answer, desired work product and questions about cost, exclusions and follow-up.

How should I compare proposals?

Use the same decision and assumptions. Compare written services, total charges, responsibilities, exclusions and what you receive.

Should I check the professional before sharing account details?

Yes. Use relevant official background resources and confirm that the identity and firm match the proposal. Agree how private records will be shared.
