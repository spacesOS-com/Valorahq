What should an advisory firm check before publishing a transcript of an educational video?

Bhavya Barot

18 Oct 2026 — 8 min read

Compare the transcript with the exact recording the firm intends to publish. Check the words, speaker labels, meaningful sounds and visual information a reader needs without the video. Mark unclear passages instead of guessing, and keep corrections connected to the source version. A readable transcript is useful only if it still represents the recording accurately.

This guide proposes an editorial checking process for an advisory firm's own educational recording. It starts after the firm has cleared the recording and intended audience through its appropriate process. It does not authorize publishing a private conversation, decide legal retention duties or establish that anyone received advice or gave consent. The examples below are invented editing situations, not accounts of actual client work.

Key takeaways

  • Compare against the final recording, not an earlier script or a different edit.
  • Treat automatic text as a draft that needs a listening check.
  • Keep uncertain words and speaker identities visibly unresolved.
  • Add important visual context as a labeled description, not invented speech.
  • Recheck the published transcript after an approved correction.

What exactly is being checked?

A transcript and a summary do different jobs. A transcript represents what the recording communicates. A summary selects and condenses that material. If an editor rewrites a speaker's explanation into a new argument, the result should not quietly keep the label "transcript."

W3C's Web Accessibility Initiative describes a basic transcript as a text version of speech and the non-speech audio information needed to understand the content. A descriptive transcript also includes the visual information needed to understand a video. [1] That distinction matters when a presenter points to a slide without reading it aloud.

Keep this review separate from checking whether the educational claims themselves are supported. A transcript can faithfully reproduce an outdated statement. Correct transcription does not make that statement current, and updating a claim in the text alone would no longer faithfully represent the recording. Send that conflict to the content owner rather than solving it through an unmarked rewrite.

Which recording should sit beside the draft?

Identify the actual source version before comparing sentences. A rehearsal, a script, an editor preview and the released recording can contain different words. Record the source reference, transcript version and person responsible for settling discrepancies in the firm's appropriate working record.

Open the intended recording and check its beginning and end as well as its title. A shared filename does not settle which edit it contains. If a section was removed from the video, text copied from an older transcript may now include material the reader cannot find in the recording.

Use timestamps as locating aids when they help. W3C notes that timestamps in transcripts are optional and can be unnecessary clutter; they do not need the granularity of caption timestamps. [1] A private review note can locate a disputed passage without turning every line of the public transcript into a timed cue.

How should automatically generated words be reviewed?

Listen while reading the draft. Do not treat fluent sentences as evidence that they match the audio. W3C's transcription guidance says automatically generated text can change the meaning of speech, including by missing a word such as "not," and says to plan time for correction. [2]

Pay close attention to negations, qualifications, names and the spoken relationship between quantities. This is an editorial priority, not a claim that checking those items alone completes the transcript. Continue through the whole recording rather than using a few clean passages to approve the rest.

For an invented example, the recording says, "This example does not include every expense," but the draft drops "not." The edit should restore the spoken sentence. It should not substitute a broader disclaimer written from memory. If the audio cannot settle the word, mark the uncertainty and ask the responsible owner to resolve it before relying on the passage.

What should happen when a word or speaker is unclear?

Preserve the difference between what can be heard and what the reviewer expects to hear. W3C's transcription guidance gives "[unintelligible]" as a way to mark speech that cannot be understood. [2] It is better to identify the gap than to fill it with a plausible financial term.

In the working note, locate the passage, describe what is uncertain and name who will decide the next step. Do not copy a real person's private information into a public editing example. This process is about a recording already intended for educational publication, not permission to expose the material behind it.

Identify speakers only as far as the evidence supports. W3C recommends identifying speakers as relevant. [2] If the recording does not establish which voice belongs to which named participant, keep that identity unresolved. A familiar voice or a transcription tool's label is not enough to settle the name.

A neutral temporary speaker label can help an editor follow turns, but it must not become an invented identity. The final public treatment needs the content owner's decision. If an unresolved passage changes the meaning, hold that passage from reuse rather than presenting a guessed answer as the speaker's words.

Which sounds and visuals belong in the text?

Include non-speech audio when it helps the reader understand the content. W3C's guidance focuses on relevant sounds and says background music need only be included when important to understanding. [2] The task is not to describe every incidental noise in the room.

Watch the recording as well as listening. A speaker might say "the condition shown here" while pointing to a slide. A transcript of those spoken words alone leaves the reader without the condition. W3C says a transcript made from video captions usually needs added visual information, including visible text and speaker identification. [1]

Describe what is actually shown and keep the addition distinct from speech. For an invented editing situation, a slide carries the words "Illustration only" while the presenter discusses a hypothetical comparison. The transcript reviewer should notice that visual qualification. They should not invent figures, conclusions or an explanation that the slide and recording do not provide.

If an important visual is unreadable in the available source, record that limit and ask for the appropriate approved source or clarification. Do not reconstruct it from what a similar presentation usually contains. The firm's separate chart guide explains how to make a chart's essential information available through text; this transcript check asks whether the recording's visual context has reached the reader at all.

Can the editor improve the wording?

Make the text easier to follow without changing what it says. W3C's transcription guidance says to transcribe accurately rather than change, adapt or add to the spoken text. It also describes circumstances where non-substantive speech can be left out for web content. [2] That is not permission to remove a qualification or repair the substance of a speaker's answer silently.

Separate an editorial clarification from the recording's words. W3C's transcript guidance says added clarifying information can be acceptable when clearly identified as outside the audio, for example in brackets or a separate section. [1] Use that distinction consistently instead of making additions look like quotations.

If a speaker made a substantive error, record the problem for the content owner. An approved explanatory note, a revised recording or a different publication decision may be appropriate, but this checklist does not choose among them. A corrected transcript should not pretend the speaker originally said something else.

A recording-to-transcript comparison record

Use the following proposed fields for a passage that needs attention. This is an original editorial aid, not an official form or evidence that a review has happened.

Source and location

Identify the recording version, transcript draft and a useful location in the recording. State whether the check used the actual intended video or another source with known limits.

Observed difference

Record the spoken words or visual context that can be established and the draft wording beside them. Separate a missing word, an uncertain sound, an unsupported speaker name and an omitted visual qualification. They may need different decisions.

Proposed treatment

Say whether the proposed change corrects transcription, adds a labeled description or leaves an unresolved gap. Do not turn an editor's interpretation into a quotation. Refer substantive content questions to the owner responsible for that explanation.

Decision and recheck

Name the responsible person and record the approved treatment through the firm's working process. Then compare the revised passage with the source again. Leave any remaining uncertainty visible in the work record instead of marking the whole transcript complete.

What should be checked in the published version?

Read the actual page or file a visitor will receive. W3C recommends making a transcript easy to find from the audio or video, such as placing it or its link near the player. It also describes logical paragraphs, headings and useful links as ways to make a transcript easier to use. [1]

Check that speaker labels remain attached to the right passages and that descriptions are distinguishable from speech on a phone as well as desktop. Follow the transcript link from the recording page and compare the served version with the reviewed copy. A saved correction has not reached the reader if that link still opens an older file.

Keep captions separate in the release check. W3C describes captions as text synchronized with the audio in a media player. [3] A corrected standalone transcript does not establish that a separate caption file was corrected or that its timing works. If both are supplied, give each its own observation rather than treating one as proof of the other.

For later reuse, return to the checked source and its limits. A transcript excerpt, article or social post is another editing decision. Do not let an unresolved passage become a confident claim simply because it has moved into a shorter format.

The bottom line

A useful transcript check keeps the recording, the words and the remaining questions together. Verify the source version, listen and watch, distinguish descriptions from speech, and trace approved corrections into the actual public artifact. Keep an uncertain word or speaker open until it is resolved; a polished sentence is not a substitute for evidence.

For visual explanations within a recording, read the related guide on making an educational chart understandable without its image.

Common questions

Is an automatic transcript ready to publish without checking?

No. W3C describes automatic text as a starting point that can need correction. Compare it with the exact recording rather than relying on fluent wording.

Should the editor guess an unclear word from context?

No. Mark the uncertainty, locate the passage and bring it to the responsible owner. W3C gives "[unintelligible]" as a marker for speech that cannot be understood.

Is a transcript the same as a summary?

No. A summary condenses material. A transcript represents the recording; identify clarifications and descriptions so they do not look like the speaker's words.

Does correcting the transcript also fix the captions?

Not necessarily. Captions are synchronized with the recording. Check the separate caption file and its timing when captions are part of the release.

Does this checklist establish consent, advice or legal recordkeeping compliance?

No. It checks an educational recording against a text version. Publication authority, substantive review and legal duties remain separate decisions.
