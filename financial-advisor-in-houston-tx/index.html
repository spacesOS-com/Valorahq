Financial advisor in Houston, TX: what to know before you hire one

Houston flips the usual financial-planning script: no state income tax, housing most households can still reach, and salaries that stretch - paired with an economy that can turn on a commodity price. The city counts 2,397,315 residents (Census Bureau, July 2025 estimate), and the Houston metro area's overall prices run about 1.4% below the national average (BEA regional price parities, 2024 - a metro-area measure, not city-only). Choosing a financial advisor here means finding someone who understands the energy cycle, concentrated stock risk, and what a no-income-tax state does to the standard playbook. This page covers all of it, including how to verify anyone before you hire them.

The Houston economy and your household balance sheet

Houston's economy is unusually concentrated in big, cyclical industries. Energy still sets the tone - oil and gas majors, oilfield services, pipeline and trading firms - and with it comes compensation built on bonuses, stock awards, and cycles that can turn fast. Around energy sits the Texas Medical Center, one of the world's largest medical complexes, a port economy that moves a meaningful share of US trade, and NASA's Johnson Space Center with its contractor ecosystem. Many Houston households earn well while depending heavily on one industry's fortunes, and often on one employer's stock.

The household numbers frame the opportunity. The median household income is $64,813 and the median owner-occupied home value is $277,800 (Census Bureau, 2020-2024) - a ratio between income and housing costs that coastal cities lost years ago. With metro prices just under the national average, a disciplined Houston household can save at rates that are genuinely hard to reach in New York or San Francisco. The typical planning conversation here is less about stretching to cover fixed costs and more about deploying the surplus well: how much company stock is too much, how to handle a bonus-heavy income, and how to build wealth that survives the energy cycle.

One more Houston reality belongs on the balance sheet: weather. The region's flood exposure is documented in detail - FEMA publishes flood studies for Harris County and maintains a public flood-map portal - which makes flood insurance, a real emergency fund, and knowing what your homeowner's policy does not cover part of basic financial hygiene here. Advisors who work with Houston families treat disaster resilience as a planning line item, not an afterthought.

No income tax: what changes and what doesn't

Texas does not levy an individual income tax, and Houston adds no local income tax on top. That single fact reshapes several standard planning moves. Roth conversions and Roth contributions are often more attractive when your marginal rate is federal-only. There is no state-level tax break to chase with retirement contributions, so the federal math drives the decision. And if you moved to Texas from a high-tax state, the raise in take-home pay is real - but states fund themselves one way or another, and Texas leans heavily on property and sales taxes. Texas also has no state estate tax, which leaves the federal rules as the relevant frame for most families.

One nuance worth knowing: many Houstonians are not lifetime Texans. If you spent part of your career in a state with an income tax - or might return to one - the timing of bonuses, equity events, and retirement moves around a relocation can change your lifetime tax bill meaningfully. That timing is fact-specific and worth mapping with a professional before the move, not after.

The property tax line: homesteads and appraisals

What Texas skips in income tax it collects elsewhere, and homeowners meet that trade directly in the mail each year. Property taxes are set locally - county, school district, city, and special districts layering together - so two similar houses a few miles apart can carry noticeably different bills. The state does offer relief on a primary residence, but none of it is automatic. Homestead exemptions reduce the taxable value of your home and generally must be applied for, and state law limits how quickly a homestead's appraised value can rise, with conditions. Appraisals can also be protested, which is why many Houston homeowners treat the annual appraisal notice as a financial to-do rather than junk mail. When you compare suburbs, the all-in tax rate belongs in the comparison next to the house price; a good advisor or realtor will run that number with you.

When your net worth rides the energy cycle

Houston's signature financial risk is concentration: the same industry that pays the salary often fills the brokerage account. An engineer at an oilfield services firm might hold restricted stock in her employer, a pension tied to the same sector, and a bonus linked to the same commodity price - triple exposure to one cycle. When energy turns down, the job, the stock, and the bonus can sag together, which is exactly when selling shares hurts most.

The counterweight is deliberate diversification, and it is harder than it sounds. Employer stock can bring trading-window limits and tax consequences. A written plan can help you compare whether, when, and how to reduce a concentrated position; the answer depends on your grants, basis, restrictions, and goals. Some Houston families add a fourth leg of exposure without noticing - mineral rights or royalty interests inherited or bought over the years - which may also be worth including in an exposure inventory and discussing with an advisor. This is the conversation a Houston advisor should lead with; if yours never raises it, that tells you something.

What a Houston advisor actually handles

Beyond generic portfolio management, the working list for a Houston advisor usually includes:

  • Concentrated stock and employer risk. Systematic diversification plans that respect trading windows, taxes, and vesting schedules - built before the cycle turns, not after.
  • Bonus and cycle planning. Smoothing a commodity-linked income into steady saving, debt payoff, and investing, with reserves sized for downturns that can last more than a quarter.
  • Retirement strategy in a no-income-tax state. Roth versus traditional decisions, conversion timing, and withdrawal sequencing when only the federal rules apply.
  • Property tax and housing strategy. Homestead exemption and appraisal-protest awareness, plus all-in rate comparisons across suburbs and districts.
  • Disaster resilience. Flood insurance gaps, emergency reserves, and document readiness as standing agenda items.
  • Physician and medical professional planning. The Medical Center employs a small city's worth of high earners with late-start careers, student debt, and practice-ownership decisions.
  • Business, mineral, and royalty interests. Coordinating family partnerships, royalty income, and operating businesses with estate and tax professionals.

If an advisor's plan for you reads like it was written for a high-tax coastal city, they have not understood the assignment.

How Houston advisors charge, and how to compare

Fee conversations in Houston often start with a project rather than a portfolio. Untangling a concentrated stock position or setting up a diversification plan is defined work, and many advisors will quote it as a flat project fee or an hourly engagement before any ongoing arrangement. Ongoing advice usually follows the standard models: a percentage of assets managed (commonly around 1% a year nationally, often lower as balances grow) or a flat annual fee. No reliable data tracks a Houston-specific average, so the useful comparisons are structural: ask each candidate what the first twelve months of work look like and what they cost, get the full fee schedule in writing, and ask whether they earn commissions on products they recommend alongside their fee. An advisor who can itemize the work is easier to compare than one who quotes a single percentage.

Vetting a Houston advisor: two free checks

Before any interview, run two searches. The first is the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure database: the advisor's name or firm pulls up registration status, employment history, disclosed disciplinary events, and the Form ADV brochure describing services, fees, and conflicts in the firm's own words. The second is the Texas State Securities Board, which regulates advisors operating in the state, publishes enforcement actions, and takes investor complaints - its public records can surface state-level issues that never reached the federal level. Neither check costs anything or takes more than a few minutes. Then, in the room: Are you a fiduciary at all times? Fee-only or fee-based? Who will I actually work with?

Questions to ask a Houston advisor in your first meeting

  • A lot of my net worth is tied to my employer and the energy cycle. How would you build a diversification plan for me, and what would it cost?
  • How does your tax planning change for clients in a no-income-tax state - Roth decisions, conversions, withdrawal sequencing?
  • Do you factor flood risk and insurance gaps into your planning?
  • How many of your clients work in energy, medicine, or engineering, and what did you set up for them?
  • Are you a fiduciary at all times, and are you fee-only? How exactly are you paid, and what would the first year of work together cost?
  • At what point would you bring in a CPA or estate attorney - say for business-sale planning or Texas estate documents - and how do you coordinate with them?

The right Houston advisor treats concentration risk, the energy cycle, and the no-income-tax playbook as the core of the plan - not as footnotes to a national template.

Common questions

How much does a financial advisor in Houston cost?

It depends on the work. Defined projects like a stock-diversification plan are often quoted flat or hourly; ongoing management is usually a percentage of assets (commonly around 1% a year nationally) or a flat annual fee. No reliable data tracks a Houston-specific average, so ask each candidate to itemize the first year of work and its cost, in writing, and compare those side by side.

Do I need a local advisor in Houston, or can I work with one remotely?

Location matters less than fit and credentials. An advisor who understands Texas taxes and the energy and medical economies can serve you well remotely; a local office is a preference, not a requirement. Verify any advisor - local or remote - through the SEC's public database before hiring.

How do I check if a Houston financial advisor is legitimate?

Run two free searches: the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site (adviserinfo.sec.gov) for registration, disciplinary history, and Form ADV, and the Texas State Securities Board for state-level enforcement and complaints. Both take minutes.

What should a financial plan in Houston account for?

At minimum: Texas's lack of an individual income tax (which reshapes Roth and conversion decisions), property taxes and homestead mechanics, employer stock concentration in the energy and medical sectors, flood risk and insurance gaps, and housing costs in a market where the median home value is $277,800. A plan built on national averages misses what actually shapes your finances here.
