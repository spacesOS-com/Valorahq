Making a shared money decision when your priorities differ

When two people have different priorities for the same money, the conversation can stall or go badly before the numbers come up. This guide is about the conversation. It uses a Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) tool on discussing money productively, then gives you an original worksheet to plan one shared decision, record what you agreed and write down what is still open. It does not say how joint accounts, property or credit work, and it does not promise any outcome. Planning a conversation is not the same as agreeing.

Key takeaways

  • The CFPB says financial choices are often emotional, and that structuring the conversation can help people make decisions and avoid becoming defensive.
  • The CFPB's tool breaks the conversation into three parts: preparation, communication while you meet, and follow-up.
  • One preparation step is to decide who gives input and who is involved in making the final decision, which the CFPB calls "voice versus vote".
  • The CFPB suggests sharing an agenda in advance, and ending the conversation by agreeing on each person's next steps and the next meeting date.
  • If old disagreements come up, the CFPB suggests setting another time to address them.

This guide is for US readers. It is educational, not individual investment, tax or legal advice. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time.

About the source

The CFPB's "Discussing money productively" tool is part of its Your Money, Your Goals companion guide for military communities, so its example conversations involve a service member and a spouse. The tool's own text speaks of important money conversations with your family, and its tips are general. This guide uses the tips, not the scripts, and the worksheet below is Valora's own.

Before the conversation

The CFPB lists these preparation tips, which are paraphrased here:

  • Set goals for the conversation. What do you want to accomplish, and what decisions need to be made?
  • Decide voice versus vote. Who will be invited to give input, and who will be involved in the final decision?
  • Notice your own values and biases. What are your needs, wants and obligations? How do you feel about the other person's?
  • Make an agenda and share it. The purpose, the goals, the key decisions and the topics.
  • Gather information in advance and share it. The CFPB asks, for example, whether you have a written budget.
  • Think about making these conversations ongoing and intentional.

During the conversation

  • Ask questions and restate what you heard to check that you have it right.
  • Keep your emotions in check. The CFPB says to state your feelings so they do not derail the conversation, and to keep a neutral or positive tone.
  • Stick to the topic. If conflict comes up, the CFPB says not to bring up old disagreements, and to set another time for old issues.
  • Recap at the end. Go over what each person will do next, write the next steps where everyone can see them and agree on another date and time to meet.

After the conversation

The CFPB says to complete the next steps you agreed to, and to make a checklist to track them.

A worksheet for one shared decision

This worksheet is Valora's own. Use one per decision. Do not write account numbers or passwords on it.

Part Person A Person B
The decision we are making, in one sentence (shared) (shared)
What matters most to me here, and why
What I am worried about
What I need to know before deciding
Shared Notes
Who gives input, and who decides? (voice versus vote)
Information to bring (statements, quotes, a written budget)
Options we considered
What we agreed
What is still unresolved
Who does what next, and by when
Next meeting date and time

An example, so the columns are clear. The decision: whether to replace a household appliance this season or wait. Person A writes that avoiding a surprise breakdown matters most. Person B writes that keeping the month's spending steady matters most. Under "unresolved," they note the repair estimate they still need. Under "next," Person B gets a second quote and they meet again that weekend. This example is invented and shows no figures.

When you disagree

A worksheet does not make two people agree. If you do not agree, you can write down the unresolved question, name who needs what information and set the next meeting. The CFPB's tool says to brainstorm where to get help with next steps if needed.

Questions for professionals

  • Which part of this decision depends on legal or tax rules, and who is the right person to ask?
  • If we have a question about a joint account, shared property or credit, who should answer it?
  • Could a financial professional help us compare options once we have named our priorities?

An attorney, tax professional or financial advisor can apply rules to your facts. This guide does not.

Bottom line

Plan the conversation before the decision. Name the decision, agree who gives input and who decides, share an agenda, bring information, restate what you hear, recap with next steps and set the next date. Write down what you agreed and what is unresolved. That structure does not ensure agreement. It gives you a place to record the decision, next steps and unresolved questions.

Common questions

How can we make a shared money decision when our priorities differ?

Plan the conversation: name the decision, agree who gives input and who decides, share an agenda and bring information. Recap with next steps and a next meeting date. See the worksheet above.

Does this tell us who owns what, or who is responsible for debts?

No. This guide does not cover joint accounts, marital property or credit liability. Ask an attorney or the institution.

What if an old disagreement comes up?

The CFPB says not to bring up old disagreements during the conversation, and to set another time to address them.

What should I send through the inquiry form?

Describe the question you want to ask. Do not submit bank statements, account numbers or other confidential financial documents.
