What should I record when I revoke an automatic bank-payment authorization?

Published October 14, 2026

Keep a separate record of what you told the company, what you told your bank or credit union, and what happened to the underlying contract or bill. Save the dates, written notices, delivery evidence, reference numbers and any confirmation or further requirement. Revoking permission to take an automatic payment from your bank account is different from cancelling a service or clearing a debt.

This US guide concerns a company taking automatic payments from a consumer bank account. It does not cover recurring card subscriptions, payments you initiate through bank bill-pay or business accounts. It is an educational action-log aid, not a message sent on your behalf, an individualized deadline calculation or a promise that a debit stops immediately or a refund will be approved.

Key takeaways

  • Separate company authorization, bank notification or stop-payment, and the contract or remaining bill.
  • Record what each party was told and keep the supporting written evidence.
  • Ask the bank about its process, confirmation requirements and any fee; do not assume a phone call finishes every step.
  • Keep another payment method and remaining obligations as questions to resolve, not as cancelled by default.
  • Review later account activity and report an unauthorized debit promptly through the bank's process.

What exactly am I trying to stop?

Start by identifying the automatic debit and the company authorized to take it. Keep the authorization or agreement you have, the transaction description and the scheduled payment information available through your private records. Do not put account numbers or a full statement into a public inquiry.

The CFPB explains that you can take away a company's permission to take automatic payments from your bank account even if you previously allowed them. Its guidance distinguishes cancelling a service from changing how you pay for it. [1] Write down which of those you intend before contacting anyone.

For example, a person might keep a service but want to pay invoices another way. Another person might intend to end the service itself. Both can involve stopping automatic debits, but the contract question is different. A fictional log should record that intent without claiming that either result has occurred.

What should the company-contact record show?

The CFPB recommends calling the company to say you are taking away permission for automatic payments, then following up in a letter or email. The company may offer an online form. The guidance also says to explain whether you are cancelling the contract or subscription or only changing the payment method. [1]

Record the company, the date and channel, and the actual request. Keep the written notice and any evidence that it was submitted or received. A reference number can help locate a request, but its existence is not the same as confirmation that the company completed it.

If the company responds, save what it says. Distinguish an acknowledgement from an effective-date confirmation, a request for more information or a statement about a remaining bill. Do not paraphrase "we received your message" as "payments have stopped."

What should the bank-contact record show?

The CFPB's general guidance recommends notifying the bank or credit union that you revoked the company's authorization, then following up in writing. It says to follow the institution's suggested process and keep close track of requests and dates. [1]

Record which company and debit the notification concerns, when the bank received the request, the channel used and the response. Save any form or written confirmation. If you are asked for another document, record the requirement, where it must go and the deadline supplied by the bank. A note saying "called bank" does not show what the bank was told or what remains needed.

The CFPB's current Regulation E page separately describes the right to stop payment of a preauthorized electronic fund transfer. It says the financial institution must be notified orally or in writing at least three business days before the scheduled transfer. The institution may require written confirmation within 14 days of an oral stop-payment order and must explain that requirement and where to send it. An oral order stops being binding after 14 days if the required confirmation is not provided. [2]

Those rules have a defined scope. Do not turn them into a universal deadline for cancelling every kind of payment or contract. If a debit is close, ask the bank how it handles the actual request rather than assuming this log makes the next payment stop in time. Keep revocation and a stop-payment order as separately described actions in your notes.

How do revocation and stop-payment differ in the log?

A revocation tells the relevant parties that the company's authorization is no longer valid. A stop-payment order is an instruction to the bank or credit union not to make a payment to the specified company from your account. The CFPB says some institutions may recommend a stop-payment order too, and generally charge fees for those orders. [1]

Record the bank's actual response instead of marking either action complete because you intended it. Ask what a stop-payment order covers, whether written confirmation is required and what it costs under the institution's process. Preserve a fee quoted to you as a quoted fee, not a universal price.

The official interpretation on the CFPB's Regulation E page explains that, once notified the authorization is no longer valid, the institution must block future payments for the particular debit from the designated payee-originator. It may confirm that you informed that party, including by requiring a copy of the written revocation within 14 days of oral notification; without required confirmation, it may honor subsequent debits. [2] This is another reason to record the actual confirmation requirement rather than assuming no further step exists.

This guide does not submit either request or establish that an institution received it. A draft, a sent notice, a receipt and the institution's response are different evidence.

What happens to the contract and future bills?

Stopping the automatic payment does not cancel what you owe. The CFPB says to cancel an ongoing service contract with the company separately when that is your intention. It also says a loan still needs to be paid through another method if automatic payments are cancelled. [1]

Keep the contract question in its own part of the log. Record what you asked to cancel, which terms or response explain the result, and any final bill or amount still disputed. Do not assume that the bank can decide whether the company's contract ended.

If the relationship continues, ask how future payments will be made and when the next bill is due. If you dispute a bill, record the dispute separately from stopping the debit. This guide does not decide whether the bill is valid or whether a cancellation fee applies.

A private action-log structure

Use an entry for each communication rather than combining all contact into one completed checkmark. These are proposed record fields, not a required regulatory form.

Party and purpose

Identify the company or institution contacted and whether the request was revocation, bank notification, stop-payment or contract cancellation. Keep the actual wording or submitted form privately.

Time and evidence

Record the date, channel, notice copy and submission or receipt evidence. Distinguish the time you sent a message from the time the other party confirmed receiving it.

Response and next requirement

Save the response and its limits. Record an additional form, confirmation address or deadline exactly as supplied, and who still needs to act. Do not mark the request resolved while a stated confirmation step is open.

Remaining obligations

Identify the next bill or other payment question, any alternative method still to arrange and any contract issue awaiting the company's answer. A stopped debit is not evidence of a zero balance.

Later activity

Record a later debit and the evidence of the earlier request. Keep the transaction and reporting steps separate from any promised refund or estimated processing time.

What if another debit appears?

The CFPB recommends telling the bank or credit union right away about a payment you did not authorize or one made after authorization was revoked. It explains that timely notification matters to dispute rights. [1] Do not wait for this organizing exercise to be perfect before asking the institution about its process.

Keep the statement entry and the earlier notice evidence. Record when you reported the debit, what the bank requested and the response. This guide does not decide whether the transaction qualifies as an error, calculate a reporting deadline for your case or promise recovery.

For a fictional example, a person saves a company revocation notice and bank notification, then sees a later debit. The log points to those records and records the new report. It does not label the account refunded merely because the report was filed.

The bottom line

Preserve who was told what, when, the evidence and any confirmation step. Keep the bank-payment authorization separate from the underlying service, contract and debt. An action log helps you follow the request and identify unfinished work; it is not proof of an instant stop, a cancelled contract or a guaranteed refund.

Common questions

Does revoking automatic payment cancel my contract?

No. The CFPB distinguishes stopping automatic debits from cancelling an ongoing service or the amount you owe. Resolve the contract question with the company separately.

Is notifying the company the same as notifying the bank?

No. Keep each contact and its response separately. The CFPB's guidance describes contacting both and retaining written follow-up.

Can the bank require written confirmation?

For a stop-payment order within the regulation's scope, the bank may require written confirmation within 14 days of oral notification and must explain the requirement and address. Ask about the actual process and preserve the instructions.

Will a stop-payment order be free?

Do not assume that. The CFPB says banks and credit unions generally charge fees. Ask your institution about the fee for the actual request.

Does this log guarantee a refund after a later debit?

No. Report the debit promptly through the bank's process and preserve your evidence. The log does not determine the outcome or replace applicable reporting requirements.
