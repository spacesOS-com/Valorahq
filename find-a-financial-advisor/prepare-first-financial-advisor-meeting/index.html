How to prepare for a first financial-advisor meeting

Prepare a short decision brief, a private list of relevant records and the questions you need answered about the professional's services. You do not need to turn a first conversation into a complete transfer of account documents. Ask what the meeting covers, what it costs and how private information would be shared before you send it.

This checklist is for a consumer preparing a conversation, not an advisor designing an intake process. It does not tell you to hire someone, open an account or agree to a recommendation. Its purpose is to help you understand what is being offered and what remains undecided.

Key takeaways

  • Name the decision and date that matter to you.
  • Prepare a record inventory without sending confidential documents.
  • Ask about services, costs, responsibilities and exclusions.
  • Leave with a clear next step, not an assumed commitment.

Write a decision brief you can explain

Use one sentence to describe the question. "I want a written comparison of these two options" is more specific than "I need help with everything." Add the date the question matters and the information you already have. If you do not know which service you want, say that rather than pretending the scope is settled.

Separate actual records from expectations. An offer, proposed sale or estimated balance may be important without being confirmed cash. The household income change guide keeps payment evidence and timing distinct. A first meeting can clarify which records a later assignment needs; it does not turn every estimate into a fact.

The when-to-hire guide addresses the earlier decision about exploring paid help. This checklist begins after you have chosen to have a conversation, without assuming you have chosen an ongoing relationship.

Ask what the meeting includes

Before the conversation, ask whether it is an introductory discussion, a paid review or part of a proposed service. Ask about the duration, any charge, who will attend and what you should prepare. If the professional requests documents, ask why those particular records are needed and how they will be exchanged securely.

The SEC's relationship-summary guide explains that Form CRS describes services, fees, conflicts and other information about a firm. Use the relevant firm's documents to prepare questions, not to assume every person called an advisor does the same work. Ask which proposed service the meeting concerns.

Keep a question open when the documents do not explain it. You can ask for the missing written scope or fee schedule instead of inventing an answer from a website biography. A professional's reassuring description is not the same as a defined deliverable.

Make a private record inventory

List the records that relate to your question, their dates and what they show. At this stage the list may be enough: a payroll record, plan document, account statement or proposed agreement. Do not paste account numbers, tax returns or confidential contracts into a public inquiry.

Mark a record as missing when it is missing. If two documents disagree, note the difference and who can explain it. The checklist should make uncertainty visible, not hide it behind a neat summary. Ask whether the professional's work includes obtaining or interpreting the missing record.

Keep six fields privately: decision and date; relevant records; confirmed facts versus assumptions; questions about services and costs; privacy and sharing method; and agreed next step. The blank CSV is an original preparation aid, not a suitability test or a required intake form.

Bring questions that test the proposal

The FINRA working-with-an-investment-professional guide encourages defining your needs, discussing fees and asking how the person is paid. Prepare those questions alongside the exact work you want. You do not have to settle every financial decision during an introductory meeting.

Ask what you would receive, who would do the work, which responsibilities remain yours and what is outside the service. Ask how charges are calculated and which costs are separate. If the conversation moves to a different service than the one you expected, ask for the changed scope in writing.

This page does not repeat the compensation classifications in the fee-only and commission guide. Use that guide for its distinct questions. Here, the meeting preparation task is to record the answer and the document supporting it.

Check the identity and agree the next step

The SEC's Ask and Check guide points to official background resources. Confirm the person and firm match the proposed relationship before sharing private records. A familiar title or a polished profile is not a substitute for checking the relevant record.

End the meeting by writing down what happens next, who owns it and whether another charge or agreement is involved. Keep a request for more information separate from agreement to a service. If you need time to understand a proposal, preserve that question rather than calling the decision complete.

Common questions

What should I prepare for a first meeting?

Prepare a decision brief, a private record inventory and questions about the service, costs, responsibilities, exclusions and next step.

Do I need to send every account document in advance?

No. Ask which records are needed, why they are needed and how they would be exchanged securely. Keep confidential documents out of a public inquiry.

Does an introductory conversation commit me to a service?

Do not assume it does. Ask what the meeting covers, whether it costs anything and which agreement or next step is proposed.

What if a record is missing or two documents disagree?

Note the gap or disagreement and who can explain it. Ask whether obtaining or interpreting the record is included in the work.

What should I record at the end?

Record the next step, its owner, the supporting written scope and whether another charge or agreement is involved.
