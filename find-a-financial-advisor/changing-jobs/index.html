Changing jobs: which records to collect and which questions to ask both employers

Changing jobs is two handoffs, not one event. One employer closes out your pay, tax forms and coverage; the other opens payroll, withholding and enrollment. Collect the records you will lose access to, confirm each handoff in writing and keep every uncertain item attached to the contact who can resolve it. Then bring a specific question, not a shoebox, to any professional review.

Key takeaways

  • Gather old-employer records before system access ends, and confirm where your final tax form will be sent.
  • Treat health coverage between jobs as a dated question with a responsible plan contact, not an assumption.
  • Reset withholding from actual pay records after the change, using the IRS estimator rather than a guess.
  • This page does not decide what to do with an old retirement account; that is a separate reviewed question.

This guide is for US readers preparing a move between employers. It organizes records and questions; it does not recommend a benefit election, an account transfer or a tax position. Valora is not a registered investment adviser and is not arranging advisor matches or introductions at this time. Keep your worksheet private and do not send statements, contracts or account details through an inquiry.

Treat the change as two handoffs, not one event

The old employer's side includes final pay, tax forms, benefit notices and the contact who can explain them later. The new employer's side includes payroll setup, withholding, enrollment windows and the documents that describe them. A question that belongs to one side cannot be answered by the other.

Write down which side owns each open item before your last day. Access to an old employer's portals, email and document libraries often ends at separation, and rebuilding a record afterward is slower than saving it while you still have access. A private list made now is cheaper than a reconstruction in February.

Keep this page's scope narrow. It covers records, questions and handoffs. It does not cover what to do with a retirement account at a former employer, and it does not compare job offers. Those are separate questions with their own documents and reviewers.

Collect old-employer records before access ends

Save your final pay stubs and any year-to-date summaries while you can still download them. Confirm the mailing address and email the employer will use for your Form W-2, and record the HR or payroll contact who can correct it later.

The IRS tells readers who are missing a W-2, or whose form is incorrect, to contact the employer first. Its Topic 154 page currently says that if the form is still missing by the end of February, you may call the IRS for help, and it describes Form 4852 as a substitute you can use to file. [1] Keep the pay stubs: they are your own evidence of wages and withholding if a form arrives late or wrong.

Collect the benefit notices that apply to you rather than the whole portal. If you had group health coverage, the plan's notices matter more than a benefits overview page. Record the plan's name, the administrator's contact and any document that states what happens to your coverage after your last day.

Keep health coverage as a dated question, not an assumption

The Department of Labor advises workers to ask about a potential new employer's plan before switching: what it covers, the premium, whether your doctors participate and whether a waiting period applies before enrollment. It notes a waiting period may generally last up to 90 days from the date you become eligible. [2] Ask the new employer's HR which of these applies to you and where it is written.

Federal COBRA rules may let you continue the old plan temporarily. The Department of Labor's COBRA FAQ says plans sponsored by private employers with 20 or more employees are generally covered, that you usually pay the full premium plus two percent, that the plan must send an election notice within 44 days after a qualifying event, and that you then have 60 days to elect and 45 days to make the first payment. For a job change, continuation coverage can last up to 18 months. [3] The Labor Department's COBRA topic page adds that this continuation right covers transitions between jobs, not only job loss. [4]

COBRA is not the only route, and the same FAQ lists alternatives with their own windows: a spouse's, parent's or dependent's group plan may offer special enrollment within 30 days of losing job-based coverage, the Health Insurance Marketplace allows plan selection within 60 days, and Medicaid and CHIP accept applications at any time. [3] Each route has its own dates. Record the date that applies to you from the plan or program itself, not from this page.

Reset withholding from actual pay records

A new employer means a new Form W-4. The IRS says to complete Form W-4 so your employer can withhold the correct federal income tax, and to consider a new one each year and whenever your personal or financial situation changes. [5] A job change is exactly that situation.

The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator is the supported way to check the number against reality. It asks for your most recent pay stubs - and your spouse's, if you file jointly - plus your latest return if you have other income, takes about 25 minutes, does not save your information and can generate a pre-filled W-4 to give your employer or payroll system. The IRS recommends checking withholding every January and after major life changes, and it lists a new job as one of them. [6]

Treat the estimator's output as an estimate to verify against actual pay stubs once they arrive, not as a permanent setting. If the change happens late in the year, the IRS notes you may need to adjust again in December so the following year starts correctly. [6]

Confirm each employer handoff in writing

A handoff is confirmed when you hold a record or a written answer, not when someone mentions it in a meeting. For each open item, write down the responsible contact on the correct side: old-employer payroll for the W-2 address and final pay records, the plan administrator for coverage documents, new-employer HR for enrollment dates and payroll for the withholding setup.

Final pay timing and payout rules vary by state and by employer policy, so this page deliberately gives no deadline. Ask the old employer's payroll which schedule applies to you and where it is written, and keep the answer with your records.

If two statements disagree - a portal message and an email, a summary and a plan document - preserve both and ask the responsible contact to reconcile them. Do not silently keep the version that looks more favorable.

Use a private records-and-handoff worksheet

Copy these six fields into private notes. The worksheet organizes evidence; it is not a benefits election, a tax filing aid or an instruction to move any account.

1. Old-employer records saved

Record each document you saved, its date and where it is stored. Include final pay stubs, year-to-date summaries and benefit notices. Private note: record [blank]; date [blank]; stored location [blank].

2. Where the W-2 will go

Record the address and email the old employer will use, the contact who can correct them and the date you confirmed them. Private note: address confirmed [blank]; payroll contact [blank]; confirmation date [blank].

3. Coverage decision and its dates

Record your current plan, the coverage routes you are comparing, and the decision date for each route from the plan or program itself. Label unanswered questions. Private note: plan [blank]; route [blank]; decision date and its written source [blank].

4. New-employer enrollment items

Record each enrollment question, the document that describes it and the HR or payroll contact who can confirm it. Private note: item [blank]; document [blank]; contact [blank].

5. Withholding check

Record the date you ran the estimator, the pay stubs you used and whether the result produced a new W-4. Note the pay date when you will compare the estimate against an actual stub. Private note: estimator date [blank]; new W-4 submitted [blank]; verification pay date [blank].

6. Open items and owners

List every unresolved question, the side that owns it and what would count as an answer. Private note: question [blank]; owning contact [blank]; answer evidence [blank].

Where this page stops

Some neighbors of this page answer different questions, and this page deliberately does not repeat them. When the new job's offer, first paycheck and onboarding documents are the issue, use the starting-a-new-job guide. When the change alters household cash flow and bill timing, the household-income-change guide separates confirmed payments from expected ones. If the interval between roles becomes a period without pay rather than a direct move, the career-break guide organizes that situation.

What to do with a retirement account at a former employer is a separate reviewed question. Record it as an open item and ask an appropriately scoped professional to review the actual plan documents, options and consequences. This page neither recommends nor rules out any account action.

Conclusion

A job change goes better when the records leave with you. Save old-employer documents before access ends, confirm where the W-2 will go, treat coverage as a dated question with a named plan contact, and reset withholding from real pay records after the move. Keep each uncertain item attached to the employer side that owns it, and bring the unresolved questions - not assumptions - to any professional review.

Common questions

What records should I collect from my old employer before I leave?

Save final pay stubs and year-to-date summaries, confirm the address and email for your Form W-2, keep benefit notices that apply to you, and record the HR or payroll contact who can correct or reissue documents later.

Does this page tell me what to do with my old retirement account?

No. That is a separate reviewed question. Record it as an open item and ask an appropriately scoped professional to review the actual plan documents, options and consequences before any decision.

How long can COBRA coverage last after changing jobs?

The Department of Labor's COBRA FAQ says continuation coverage after a termination or reduction in hours can last up to 18 months, with extensions in limited situations such as disability or a second qualifying event. Your plan administrator confirms what applies to you. [3]

When should I update my Form W-4?

The IRS says to consider a new W-4 each year and whenever your personal or financial situation changes, and its estimator page lists a new job as a reason to check withholding. Verify any estimate against your actual pay stubs. [5] [6]
